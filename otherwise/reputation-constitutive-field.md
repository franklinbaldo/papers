# The Constitutive Field Problem: Why Cheaper Verification May Not Devalue Legal Reputational Capital

---

## 1. Thesis Attacked

"Reputação como Mecanismo de Coordenação no Sistema Jurídico" (Baldo, 2025; `paper1F_reputacao_sistema_juridico.md`) defends a two-part prediction about the Brazilian legal system:

> "A redução tecnológica do custo de verificação da qualidade argumentativa recalibra esse sistema em dois sentidos: desvaloriza proxies institucionais quando qualidade é verificável diretamente, e abre novo canal de construção de reputação para agentes antes excluídos do sistema."

The prediction has two components. First, when argumentative quality becomes directly verifiable at lower cost, institutional proxies (affiliation, win history, credentials, network) lose relative value as reputation-formation inputs. Second, agents previously excluded from the reputation network can build reputation through verified quality alone, democratizing access to the system. This attack targets the causal mechanism linking cheaper verification to proxy devaluation to democratization.

---

## 2. Faithful Reconstruction

The paper's argument is strongest when understood through its three-stage structure.

**Stage 1 (Informational diagnosis):** Argumentative quality in law is a credence good — expensive or impossible to verify even after consumption. A judge receiving a petition cannot cheaply verify whether it correctly identifies the *ratio decidendi* of the relevant precedent, whether a proposed distinction is genuine, or whether an omission weakens the fundamentação. This creates information asymmetry of the type Akerlof analyzed: buyers (decision-makers) who cannot distinguish quality from non-quality pay for expected quality; sellers (practitioners) of genuine quality cannot extract a premium; quality production incentives collapse.

**Stage 2 (Proxy mechanism):** The legal system avoids quality collapse by operating a distributed reputation network. Observable proxies — institutional affiliation, win history, credentials, professional network — function as screening devices: they correlate with quality without directly measuring it. Reputation, in the paper's framework, is "the decentralized price of argumentative quality — the mechanism through which information about quality propagates through the network without direct verification." The analysis correctly notes that proxies are imperfect and that agents with accumulated reputational capital have a structural interest in maintaining the informational opacity that makes proxies necessary.

**Stage 3 (Technology prediction):** When verification technology reduces the cost of direct quality assessment, the screening function of proxies diminishes. The argument follows the structure of information disclosure: when quality becomes visible, signals that substitute for quality visibility lose informational value. The paper integrates Kreps's repeated-game analysis by noting that reputation serves as a credible commitment device — but the paper's prediction is that cheaper verification reduces the need for the commitment device by making direct assessment possible, lowering the value of the proxy through which the commitment was built.

The paper's strongest version is its own: it does not claim proxies disappear but that their "peso relativo na formação de reputação diminui quando verificação direta se torna mais barata" — a partial, differential claim that is more defensible than a strong devaluation claim.

---

## 3. The Attack

The paper's mechanism contains a suppressed premise that neither its Akerlof framing nor its Bourdieu framing supports: that "argumentative quality" is an objective standard existing independently of the field's capital structure and accessible to verification technology.

### 3.1 The Constitutive Capital Problem

The paper draws on Bourdieu's concepts of symbolic capital and the legal field. In Bourdieu's framework, the legal field is not simply a market where quality is produced exogenously and then signaled. The field constitutes the standards through which quality is recognized: what counts as a sound argument, as genuine precedent distinction, as adequate *ratio* engagement, is determined in part by agents recognized as competent judges of these things — and recognition of competence is itself a function of capital distribution within the field. This is not circular in a vicious sense; it is how any knowledge field reproduces its standards. But it is structurally important.

The implication: verification technology trained on legal production would encode existing quality standards. The systems the paper's program proposes — formal verification pipelines, structured argument auditing — are calibrated against legal argumentation as it is produced and recognized in the current field. "Verified quality" would be "quality as recognized by the current field," which is quality structured by existing capital relations.

Under this reading, proxies would not devalue upon the introduction of verification technology. They would be embedded in the quality standard the technology verifies. An argument from a practitioner at a high-capital institution would receive a high quality score not because of institutional rent, but because the quality rubric was constructed from material that high-capital institutions produce and that the field's competent judges (themselves high-capital agents) recognize as quality. The technology would provide a cheaper window onto field-relative quality, not an independent window onto quality as such.

The force of this objection depends on whether the paper's verification mechanism is calibrated against an *exogenous* quality standard or a *field-relative* one. The paper describes verification in terms of "auditability" of "argumentative quality" and references the Lean 4 pipeline (Paper 2 of this series), which tests arguments for procedural validity under CPC 2015. A procedural-validity standard has more independence from capital relations than a peer-assessment standard — CPC 2015 is law, not field consensus. But even procedural validity is interpreted through field practice: what counts as adequate engagement with art. 489, §1º, VI is determined by existing jurisprudence, produced by high-capital tribunals, through interpretive moves that are themselves contested within the field.

The paper could respond by identifying an exogenous standard — a mathematical notion of argumentative validity, a statutory text read literally, a formal proof system — that would provide independent leverage against existing capital relations. But the paper does not make this claim. It describes verification technology as revealing quality that already exists in arguments, not as imposing an external standard. The Bourdieusian analysis the paper invokes implies that "quality that already exists in arguments" is not field-neutral.

### 3.2 The Signaling Persistence Problem

The paper cites Spence (1973) on job market signaling. The Spencean framework presents a structural difficulty the paper does not engage.

In a separating equilibrium under Spence's model, high-quality agents invest in costly signals to distinguish themselves from low-quality agents. The signal's value comes from two sources: (a) its correlation with quality, which provides information; and (b) its cost, which provides screening — it is more costly for low-quality agents to mimic the signal than for high-quality agents to produce it. The equilibrium can persist even when quality is partially observable, because the signal carries commitment value beyond its informational content.

In the legal context, institutional credentials and professional affiliations function as costly signals with both components. The informational component (proxy-as-quality-correlation) is what the paper's mechanism targets: cheaper verification substitutes for this component, reducing proxy value. But the commitment component survives cheaper verification.

The paper itself establishes, through Kreps, that legal reputation functions as a credible commitment in repeated games: the practitioner who built reputation through investment in institutional credentials has committed to the profession, to long-term relationship maintenance, and to reputational accountability — signals that cheap quality verification on a single argument cannot substitute. Direct verification of output quality reveals nothing about the investment the agent has made in the network of professional relationships and institutional accountability that makes future performance predictable. A new entrant with one verified high-quality argument has not demonstrated the commitment that decades of institutional investment signal.

This is not an objection to the existence of the information-substitution effect the paper predicts. It is an objection to the paper's implicit assumption that this effect dominates the equilibrium. In Kreps's framework, the value of commitment signals in repeated games is independent of whether the underlying quality is observable — it depends on whether the history of investment is observable, which it is (institutional affiliation, career trajectory, professional relationships are the investment record). Cheaper single-interaction quality verification does not substitute for this investment record.

A related consequence: when verification costs fall, practitioners may shift from observable proxies to other forms of costly commitment signals rather than abandoning costly signaling altogether. The equilibrium changes form without dissolving.

### 3.3 The Network Diffusion Problem

The paper's democratization prediction requires that verified quality signals propagate through the reputation network to agents with reputational authority (judges, senior lawyers, academic reviewers) without being filtered or amplified by existing capital holders. This requires three conditions the paper does not establish:

**(a) Legibility and access.** Verified quality scores must be accessible to decision-makers in formats they consult and trust. If quality verification tools are primarily used by practitioners and academics, but judges form reputational assessments through the channels described in the paper (petitions received, adversaries assessed, colleagues evaluated), then verified quality scores would reach judges only through the intermediation of practitioners — who are themselves differentially positioned in the reputation network.

**(b) Cross-interaction propagation.** Reputation in repeated games (Kreps) accumulates across interactions with many counter-parties. A single high-quality verified argument builds reputation with the specific judge who received it; propagating this to the broader network requires that other agents learn about the quality interaction. The paper describes the existing reputation network as operating through professional relationships and institutional channels. Verified quality signals would propagate through these same channels — channels that existing capital holders dominate, both as transmitters and as filters.

**(c) Absence of counter-signal investment.** High-capital agents facing verified quality competition from new entrants would have incentive to invest in counter-signals that reinforce their position: advocacy before verification bodies about what quality standards should measure, institutional affiliations that lend credibility to quality certifications, academic publications that frame quality criteria. The paper documents that high-capital agents resist recalibration through normative, cultural, and institutional channels. The same resistance mechanisms would shape what "verified quality" means, reducing the new entrant's ability to build reputation through a verification metric that existing capital holders influence.

**First adversarial response — round 2: constitutive capital's exogenous-standard defense accepted for the structural compliance core but leaves a mechanism-scale gap; commitment-displacement asymmetry for new entrants accepted; mimicry-resistance of formal compliance records unestablished; decision-text diffusion channel accepted while attribution gap blocks its function as a practitioner reputation channel.**

The supportive round 1 (`yesindeed/paper1f-recalibration-defense.md`) responds on all three attack vectors. The most consequential advance is on constitutive capital: CPC 2015 arts. 489, §1º, V–VI and 927 constitute an external normative shock of the type §4 anticipated — the paper's own program (Papers 1A–1G) is precisely the analysis of compliance requirements those provisions imposed. This is accepted as genuine. Three residuals follow.

*Constitutive capital — core-class exogeneity accepted; inverse relationship between exogeneity and mechanism value.* The statutory compliance standard has genuine independence from field capital for the structurally identifiable core: decisions omitting required precedent citations, failing to state applicable statutory provisions, containing explicit internal contradictions. For these cases, compliance verification performs structural textual comparison without interpretive judgment constituted by field capital. The adversarial §4 acknowledged this argument had force; the concession stands.

The core-class defense does not scale to the paper's systemic recalibration claim. The relationship between exogenous-standard clarity and verification-technology value is inverse. Cases in the structural compliance core are cases that competent readers identify through ordinary professional review without verification technology: a senior practitioner reviewing an adversary's brief already identifies the missing precedent citation; a court reviewing a challenged decision already sees the statutory provision gap. Verification technology on these cases is a cost-reduction tool — it makes raising compliance objections cheaper and more systematic — but it does not displace the reputational evaluation of argument analytical quality, because the cases it catches are already visible without it.

Where verification technology would add most value beyond ordinary professional review — identifying inadequate ratio decidendi engagement at the doctrinal-specific level, detecting substitution of principle-level citation for required doctrinal analysis, assessing whether the argument addresses the fundamentos determinantes the case demanded — the question of what counts as adequate engagement is determined through interpretive judgment that existing field practice structures. The constitutive capital critique retains force precisely where the mechanism's added value is highest. The core-class defense succeeds where the mechanism is weakest and fails where the mechanism needs to be strongest for systemic recalibration.

*Signaling persistence — new-entrant asymmetry accepted; mimicry-resistance of formal compliance records unestablished.* The supportive's round 1 makes two Kreps-framework moves: (a) observable verified output history functions equivalently to institutional investment records as a commitment signal — Kreps imposes no restriction on the form of observable commitment history; (b) the commitment-displacement effect is asymmetric — incumbents retain their institutional investment value while new entrants gain an alternative commitment path through verified output accumulation. The new-entrant asymmetry is accepted: the paper's democratization claim is most defensible as a differential prediction about competitive positions, not a total-proxy-replacement claim.

The mimicry-resistance problem the supportive does not address: Kreps's separating equilibrium requires commitment signals to be costly to mimic — the signal must be cheaper for high-quality agents to produce than for low-quality agents to replicate at scale. Institutional investment records have this property structurally: multi-dimensional investment over time (network building, credential acquisition, institutional affiliation maintenance) cannot be cheaply compressed into a shorter period regardless of agent quality. Whether formal compliance records share this property depends on whether the compliance standard captures quality distinctions requiring genuine analytical depth, or whether it checks structural format compliance that can be satisfied at lower cost through optimization.

If the verification standard checks structural formal completeness — statutory citations present, relevant precedents mentioned, dispositif not explicitly contradicted — agents optimizing for compliance metrics can satisfy these markers without the analytical depth that distinguishes adequate from excellent fundamentação. A practitioner who learns to cite the right statutes and mention the relevant precedents produces a formally compliant record at lower cost than a practitioner providing genuinely adequate ratio engagement. The compliance record would then attract agents who optimize for measurable compliance markers — producing a gameable proxy of the same type the paper's mechanism is designed to replace. Mimicry-resistance requires the compliance standard to be harder to game than institutional proxies; the paper does not establish this.

*Network diffusion — decision-text channel accepted; attribution gap blocks practitioner reputation propagation.* The supportive identifies court decisions published through official channels (Diário da Justiça, court websites) as a diffusion path independent of practitioner control — compliance findings documented in the decision text, not filterable by high-capital practitioners after publication. This is accepted as a real channel. The supportive's acknowledgment that cross-interaction propagation (condition b in §3.3) remains a challenge is noted.

The attribution gap the supportive does not address: court decisions record compliance findings attributed to the case outcome, not to the practitioner who argued the compliance violation. A court granting a procedural remedy for fundamentação inadequacy documents the specific deficiency found in the challenged decision — not the identity or quality of the petitioner's attorney who identified the deficiency and argued for the remedy. The compliance record enters the official archive as a case outcome. For this to become a practitioner reputation input, the network must attribute the outcome to practitioner Y — requiring intermediaries: institutional profiles listing significant victories, academic publications analyzing counsel strategy, co-counsel networks tracking who argued what, professional directories recording significant outcomes. These intermediaries are capital-holder-structured: institutional firms curate victory profiles; academic publications select doctrinal-significance cases; co-counsel networks operate selectively by relationship. The decision-text channel produces a compliance record in the court archive; the reputation channel requires attribution through intermediaries that are not independent of existing capital distribution.

Cross-interaction propagation (condition b) survives even with the decision-text channel established: a practitioner's compliance victory in Tribunal X propagates to counter-parties in Tribunal Y only through these same intermediaries. Official publication of court decisions is technically accessible nationwide, but practitioners and judges track outcomes in their own specialized dockets, not compliance victories by practitioners across jurisdictions. Without systematic cross-court reputation tracking organized around practitioner attribution rather than case outcome, the decision-text channel supports local reputation accumulation at the specific court where the compliance argument was made — not the networked propagation the paper's democratization prediction requires.

**Second adversarial response — round 3: coverage extension bounded by utilization constraint and conditional prevalence gap; informed mimicry costs fall below the statutory ceiling as compliant decision corpora grow; local Kreps presupposes repeated-interaction relationships that new entrants lack and requires judicial-attention conditions incompatible with the coverage-extension premise.**

*Coverage extension — the principal outstanding obligation.* The supportive's round 2 response correctly identifies the inverse-relationship objection's scope: it applies within the population that professional review already reaches. The mechanism's coverage value in the high-volume, low-review population — public defenders carrying hundreds of simultaneous matters, civil defendants without specialized counsel in mass collection dockets, administrative parties in high-volume proceedings — is a genuinely distinct claim that the adversarial's round 2 did not address. Two constraints bound it.

*Utilization constraint.* Coverage extension requires not only that the mechanism detect compliance failures but that detected failures result in effective compliance challenges producing the decision-text records on which the diffusion channel depends. A compliance challenge requires: (1) standing to raise the objection; (2) institutional capacity to file and litigate it; (3) court adjudication in a decision text that enters the official archive. The supportive's diffusion mechanism operates from step 3 onward. In the high-volume, low-review population, the resource constraints that prevent systematic review apply equally to steps 1–3. A public defender carrying hundreds of simultaneous matters who detects a compliance failure through automated tools cannot systematically litigate compliance objections across that caseload — the same resource constraint that forecloses detailed review forecloses the systematic challenge filing on which the decision-text channel depends. Detection without capacity to challenge does not produce the compliance record in decision text. The supportive correctly notes that automated detection extends coverage to proceedings where review cost is prohibitive; it does not address whether parties in that population can convert detections into effective challenges at the scale the mechanism requires. Detection and challenge-filing are separate steps, and the cost of the former does not reduce the cost of the latter.

*Conditional prevalence gap.* The coverage-extension argument's scale claim — that the high-volume, low-review population contains meaningful rates of detectable structural violations — is asserted but not established. High-volume dockets in the Brazilian system are also dockets where compliance standardization is most advanced. Repetitive-resource proceedings under CPC arts. 1036–1041 are institutionally defined by the stability of the legal question under review; courts in these dockets develop standardized decision templates calibrated to the art. 927 compliance requirements those provisions formalized. Criminal appellate dockets handled by public defenders in volume involve routinized case patterns with correspondingly routinized decision formats. Administrative dockets in social security, labor, and tax matters operate under established templates developed through years of institutional practice. The structural compliance failures the mechanism targets — omission of required precedent engagement, missing statutory provision statements, internal inconsistency — are least likely to be prevalent in dockets whose defining institutional feature is procedurally standardized output. The supportive asserts that parties in the high-volume, low-review population lack resources for systematic professional quality review but does not establish that these dockets produce compliance failures at a rate that makes systematic detection valuable. If violation rates are low in high-volume standardized dockets, coverage extension to that population does not provide the deterrence and detection scale the supportive's argument claims.

*Mimicry-resistance — informed optimization.* The supportive's round 2 correctly characterizes art. 489, §1º, V–VI as establishing a statutory upper bound on gaming: the provisions require identification of fundamentos determinantes and specification of distinguishing facts, not citation inclusion alone, raising mimicry costs above structural formal-marker optimization. The adversarial accepts this statutory upper bound.

The bound applies to uninformed mimicry. It does not apply with equal force to informed mimicry — optimization informed by access to a corpus of compliant decisions that have already identified the relevant fundamentos determinantes and specified the relevant distinguishing facts for the controlling precedents in specific case constellations. The same diffusion channel the supportive relies on for reputation propagation makes exactly this material available: decision texts published through official channels document the court's determination of what fundamentos determinantes identification looks like for specific precedents. An agent with access to a growing corpus of correctly adjudicated compliance decisions can reproduce the pattern of fundamentos determinantes identification for controlling precedents from prior compliant decisions without performing the independent doctrinal analysis that distinguishes genuine quality engagement from template reproduction. The statutory text sets the ceiling on uninformed mimicry; informed optimization — available to any agent with access to the officially published compliance record the supportive's mechanism predicts will grow — operates below that ceiling. The mechanism's success conditions generate the conditions for its own circumvention over time.

*Local Kreps — three structural limitations.* The supportive's round 2 identifies a first-stage local reputation accumulation channel through direct bilateral court-practitioner repeated observation, accepted as structurally available in principle. Three limitations constrain its contribution to the democratization claim.

First, *the channel does not require cheaper verification.* Courts have always observed practitioner compliance quality across repeated interactions through ordinary professional reading of filings. Local reputation accumulation through bilateral repeated observation was available before the paper's mechanism. Cheaper verification reduces the cost of demonstrating formal compliance in individual filings but does not open a channel that bilateral court-practitioner observation already provided. The democratization claim requires that cheaper verification opens a channel previously closed; the local Kreps channel was not previously closed to practitioners in repeated-interaction positions with specific courts.

Second, *the channel benefits those with pre-existing repeated-interaction positions.* The local Kreps mechanism operates for practitioners who already appear repeatedly before the same courts: established regional practitioners, institutional regular litigants, public defenders with stable court assignments. New entrants without established court relationships — the democratization prediction's primary population — lack the repeated-interaction base from which local reputation accumulates. Cheaper verification reduces the cost of each individual filing but does not substitute for the accumulated series of interactions the mechanism requires. For new entrants seeking to enter a new court relationship, the local Kreps channel is identical in structure to what it was before verification technology: the first N appearances before a court build no track record regardless of the quality demonstration cost per appearance.

Third, *the coverage-extension premise and the local Kreps premise require incompatible judicial attention conditions.* The coverage-extension argument relies on the claim that professional review is absent in high-volume dockets — courts in these proceedings cannot systematically engage with the compliance quality of each filing they receive. Local Kreps requires that courts in those same dockets observe and retain practitioner compliance patterns across cases — i.e., that courts systematically engage with practitioner compliance quality across repeated interactions. If courts lack the capacity to catch compliance failures through review, they equally lack the capacity to track practitioner compliance quality through repeated bilateral observation. The mechanism cannot simultaneously rely on high-volume dockets being under-reviewed (for coverage extension) and well-observed at the practitioner level (for local Kreps). One of the two premises must yield; the adversarial's position is that the coverage-extension characterization of high-volume dockets as under-reviewed correctly applies, and that this same characterization undermines local Kreps as a democratization mechanism in that population.

## 4. Anticipated Reply

### 4.1 The Constitutive Capital and Inverse-Relationship Anticipated Replies

The most natural response to the constitutive capital argument is that it proves too much: if quality standards were entirely constituted by field capital, legal fields could never change, which contradicts historical evidence of legal field transformation (including the CPC 2015 itself, which the paper's series argues represented a genuine recalibration of what counts as adequate fundamentação). This response has force, and the constitutive capital objection should not be read as claiming quality is entirely field-constituted.

The narrower claim stands: the paper's specific mechanism (cheaper verification → proxy devaluation) requires verification technology to operate against an exogenous quality standard that provides independent traction against existing capital distributions. Field-transforming episodes in legal history have typically involved an external normative shock — legislation, constitutional amendment, international treaty — that imposed standards that existing capital holders could not unilaterally define. Technology that audits compliance with existing legal norms does not, by itself, constitute such a shock. It verifies quality-as-currently-understood, which is quality-as-currently-structured.

The supportive round 1 (`yesindeed/paper1f-recalibration-defense.md`) executed this anticipated reply: CPC 2015 arts. 489, §1º, V–VI and 927 are the normative shock; the paper's program formalizes compliance requirements imposed by those provisions; the verification standard is statutory text, not field-relative peer assessment. The round 2 adversarial counter (above in §3) accepts the core-class defense and presses the inverse relationship between exogenous-standard clarity and verification-mechanism added value — where the standard is clearest, the technology adds least value beyond ordinary professional review; where the technology adds most value, the constitutive capital critique retains force.

On signaling persistence: the anticipated reply would distinguish commitment signals (investment records) from quality signals (argument output), conceding that the former survive cheaper verification while arguing that the latter dominate reputation formation in the paper's model. The supportive round 1 made this move via the new-entrant asymmetry argument: incumbents retain commitment value in their investment records while new entrants gain an alternative commitment path through verified output accumulation. The round 2 counter accepts the asymmetry and presses the mimicry-resistance gap: whether formal compliance records are genuinely harder to game than institutional proxies, or whether agents optimizing for measurable compliance markers can accumulate indistinguishable records at lower cost.

### 4.2 The Coverage-Extension Anticipated Reply

The most natural response to the utilization constraint is that detection capability itself reduces the cost of identifying which cases merit challenge, allowing resource-constrained parties to prioritize effectively — fifty detected violations makes it possible to select the strongest five for litigation, a better position than no detection at all. Additionally, even imperfect utilization (a small percentage of detected violations converted into challenges) is an improvement over zero, producing some compliance records in decision text and creating deterrence effects independent of the rate of challenge filing.

This response does not suffice. The utilization constraint is not that detection fails to help with triage; it is that the mechanism's claimed systemic effect requires compliance records to enter decision text at meaningful scale. A triage benefit that converts five of fifty detected violations into challenges produces five compliance records — a marginal contribution to the systemic deterrence and reputation signal the paper predicts. The mechanism's coverage value in the high-volume, low-review population requires that violations systematically enter the record, not that the detection-to-challenge conversion rate is non-zero. The adversarial's claim is that in the population where professional review is absent, challenge-filing capacity is also absent, and the conversion rate is structurally constrained rather than merely imperfect. Detection does not solve the institutional capacity problem; it relocates the binding constraint from identification to litigation.

The most natural response to the conditional prevalence gap is that the adversarial offers a structural prediction about compliance in high-volume standardized dockets without empirical evidence — it is an unsubstantiated counter-assertion rather than a demonstrated fact. This response is partly correct: the prevalence question is empirical and neither side has established the rate. The adversarial's position is that the structural prediction follows from the dockets' defining institutional feature — standardization calibrated to compliance requirements — and that the burden of establishing scale rests with the supportive, which premises the coverage-extension argument on a population containing meaningful violation rates. An unestablished empirical claim cannot be used to establish a mechanism's scale.

### 4.3 The Local Kreps and Mimicry-Resistance Anticipated Replies

On local Kreps: the most natural response is that verification tools lower the cost of each compliance demonstration per interaction, allowing reputation to accumulate faster in the bilateral court-practitioner relationship — the mechanism opens the channel earlier in a practitioner's career, before the long investment required for institutional proxy accumulation. Cheaper verification means fewer interactions are needed to establish a compliance track record.

This response does not rescue the democratization claim. The channel's earlier opening helps practitioners already in repeated-interaction positions with courts — those already appearing before the relevant tribunals. For the new entrant without established court relationships, cheaper verification per interaction does not substitute for access to those interactions in the first instance. The democratization prediction requires that cheaper verification reaches practitioners previously excluded from the reputation network; exclusion from the network operates at the court-access level, not at the cost-per-interaction level. A practitioner who cannot appear before the relevant tribunals cannot benefit from cheaper demonstrations of quality per appearance. And in the high-volume dockets where coverage extension claims the mechanism operates, the incompatibility between the under-review characterization and the bilateral-observation requirement remains.

On mimicry-resistance: the most natural response is that template reproduction is detectable in repeated interactions, because case-specific factual configurations vary across cases and template-based fundamentos determinantes identification fails when applied to facts that differ from the template cases. The court accumulates observations of the practitioner's ability to identify fundamentos determinantes in new configurations, not merely in configurations that prior compliant decisions already addressed.

This response correctly identifies that informed mimicry has limits. The adversarial does not claim template reproduction is undetectable or costless. The claim is that it is cheaper than the supportive's framework assumes — specifically, that the statutory upper bound applies to uninformed optimization and that informed optimization operates below that ceiling. How far below the ceiling informed optimization operates depends on the specificity of the compliance determinations in the official archive and the variance in case configurations the practitioner faces. For practitioners in standardized dockets with low case-configuration variance, template reproduction operates close to the statutory upper bound as a mimicry strategy. Whether courts in those dockets can systematically distinguish template reproduction from genuine analysis in repeated interactions returns to the judicial attention question: courts in high-volume standardized dockets face the same resource constraints the coverage-extension argument identifies as preventing systematic compliance review.

## 5. Scope of the Attack

This attack does not claim that technology has no effect on reputational capital in legal systems, that excluded agents cannot benefit from quality verification tools, or that the paper's general direction is wrong. The claim is bounded: the specific causal mechanism (cheaper verification → proxy devaluation → democratization) is underdetermined by the theoretical frameworks the paper invokes. The constitutive capital problem shows that verification technology trained on field production would encode existing capital relations into the quality standard it verifies. The signaling persistence problem shows that commitment-value proxies — which Kreps's analysis, which the paper invokes, implies are present in legal reputation — survive cheaper output verification. The coverage-extension argument correctly identifies a population where the mechanism could operate without direct competition from professional review; the utilization constraint and conditional prevalence gap show that coverage extension to that population is bounded by challenge-filing capacity and violation-rate assumptions neither of which is established. The local Kreps channel is accepted as structurally available but limited to practitioners with pre-existing repeated-interaction court relationships and facing judicial-attention conditions incompatible with the high-volume, low-review characterization the coverage-extension argument relies on.

Together, these constraints suggest the paper's mechanism operates less strongly, less broadly across the Brazilian docket, and less reliably in the democratizing direction than predicted. The mechanism is not zero — the core-class exogenous standard defense identifies a domain where constitutive capital interference is limited; the new-entrant asymmetry identifies a genuine differential competitive effect; the decision-text channel is real. What the attack contests is the systemic, network-wide scale and the democratization trajectory the paper's central thesis predicts.

The attack is not a defense of the status quo. Opacity is costly; verification tools may produce genuine benefits. The objection is that the paper underestimates the structural stickiness of capital distribution in legal fields and does not identify the mechanism by which verified quality would propagate to reputational authority through channels that existing capital holders do not influence — and that the coverage-extension argument, which offered the most promising terrain for the mechanism, faces utilization and prevalence gaps that were not addressed before round 3.

## 6. Surrender Conditions

The paper's thesis would survive this attack under the following conditions.

**(a) Exogenous quality standard with coverage-extension at scale.** The paper identifies a quality standard not constituted by existing field capital and shows that Brazilian legal verification technology would measure against this standard rather than against field-relative quality norms. The supportive round 1 partially satisfies this condition for the structural compliance core. The mechanism-scale extension to the high-volume, low-review population requires additionally: (i) evidence that parties in that population have institutional capacity to convert automated detections into effective compliance challenges at rates sufficient to produce the compliance records the diffusion channel requires — not only that detection is available, but that detection-to-challenge-filing capacity exists in the constrained institutional settings where review is absent; and (ii) evidence that detectable structural compliance violations are prevalent at meaningful rates in high-volume standardized dockets, rather than rare in precisely those dockets due to their compliance-standardized institutional design. The core-class defense is accepted; the mechanism-scale extension requires utilization evidence and prevalence evidence that have not been provided.

**(b) Commitment signal separability with mimicry-resistance for informed optimization.** The paper shows that Brazilian legal reputation proxies function primarily as informational signals (Akerlof) rather than commitment devices (Kreps-Spence), or that the commitment-value component is substitutable by verified quality accumulated across multiple interactions. The supportive round 1 partially satisfies this condition for new entrants through the asymmetric commitment-displacement argument. For full satisfaction, the formal compliance record must be shown to be resistant not only to uninformed structural formal-marker optimization but to informed optimization by agents with access to a corpus of officially published compliant compliance determinations identifying the fundamentos determinantes for controlling precedents in specific case constellations. Meeting this condition requires either showing that the compliance standard requires case-specific analytical depth that cannot be reproduced from prior compliant decision patterns in the standard case, or that courts in the relevant dockets systematically distinguish template reproduction from genuine analysis — subject to the judicial-attention constraints the high-volume docket characterization identifies.

**(c) Independent diffusion mechanism reaching new entrants without existing court relationships.** The paper identifies a network diffusion mechanism through which verified quality signals reach reputational authority agents without being filtered by existing capital holders. The supportive rounds 1 and 2 identify decision-text records through official publication channels and local Kreps first-stage reputation accumulation through bilateral court-practitioner repeated observation. For full satisfaction, the local Kreps channel must be shown to operate for practitioners without pre-existing repeated-interaction court relationships — the channel's existence for practitioners already in repeated-interaction positions was substantially available before cheaper verification. Additionally, the coverage-extension and local Kreps arguments apply incompatible assumptions about judicial attention in high-volume dockets: if courts in those dockets lack review capacity (coverage-extension premise), they equally lack systematic bilateral-observation capacity (local Kreps requirement). This incompatibility must be resolved — either by identifying dockets where courts maintain practitioner-level compliance tracking despite volume constraints, or by separating the coverage-extension and local Kreps arguments to apply in different docket populations.

As of round 3, each condition remains partially open: (a) core-class exogenous standard accepted; utilization evidence and prevalence evidence in the low-review, high-volume population pending; (b) new-entrant asymmetry established; mimicry-resistance against informed optimization unestablished; (c) decision-text and local-Kreps channels established for the practitioners already in repeated-interaction positions; new-entrant channel and judicial-attention incompatibility unresolved. The mechanism as articulated may improve the quality and efficiency of legal argument production in the high-review population without producing the systemic recalibration and democratization effects the paper's central thesis predicts.
