# The Underspecified Transplant: Why Civilian Judicial Architecture Already Predicts the Direction of *Livre Convencimento* Distortion

---

## 1. Thesis Attacked

"O Livre Convencimento como Doutrinização do Patrimonialismo Judicial" (Baldo, 2026; `paper1G_livre_convencimento_patrimonialismo.md`), as formalized in the supportive paper "The Doctrinalization Mechanism" (`yesindeed/paper1g-doctrinalization-mechanism.md`), advances the following as the thesis's central diagnostic contribution:

> "The transplant hypothesis predicts distortion but not its direction; the patrimonialism thesis predicts the specific direction of the distortion." (supportive paper, §3.4)

And specifically:

> "A receiving context with a strong Roman-law tradition of systematic rule-application would be expected to produce drift toward formalism, not personal discretion. A context with collective deliberation traditions would produce drift toward conciliation procedures. The direction of the Brazilian drift — toward expanding judicial personal conviction, not toward some other reconstruction — is explained by the patrimonial habitus and not by transplant-difficulty alone." (supportive paper, §3.4)

The thesis being attacked is this directional-specificity claim: that the Watson (1974) legal transplant hypothesis cannot explain the specific direction of Brazil's *livre convencimento* distortion, and that the patrimonialism thesis adds irreducible directional explanatory content.

---

## 2. Faithful Reconstruction

The strongest form of Paper 1G's directional-specificity argument is as follows.

Watson's transplant hypothesis holds that legal rules lose context in transit; the receiving context's available interpretive resources shape how the transplanted rule is reconstructed; and the outcome will therefore differ from the source. This hypothesis correctly predicts that distortion occurred in Brazil's reception of *livre convencimento*. But the hypothesis leaves the direction of distortion underdetermined: without specifying what the receiving context's interpretive resources actually are, the hypothesis cannot predict whether the Brazilian drift would be toward personal discretion, toward formalism, toward collective deliberation, or toward some other reconstruction. The hypothesis predicts drift-from-source without predicting drift-direction.

The patrimonialism thesis fills this gap. The Brazilian legal field's patrimonial habitus — the disposition to treat judicial authority as an extension of personal authority, and to regard formal norms as optional instruments rather than binding constraints — constitutes the specific interpretive resource that shaped the direction of reception. A judge acting under a patrimonial habitus will naturally expand the liberty dimension of "free judicial conviction" (freedom from external constraint on personal judgment) and contract the rational-engagement dimension (the requirement to expose reasoning to intersubjective scrutiny). The direction toward personal-conviction expansion, and away from rational-engagement constraint, is precisely what the patrimonial habitus predicts.

The thesis is not ad hoc, because Faoro and Holanda established the patrimonialism account independently across political, bureaucratic, and economic domains. Applying it to the judicial field via Bourdieu's habitus mechanism is an extension of an independently grounded account, not an auxiliary hypothesis constructed to explain this specific case.

This is the argument being attacked. The directional-specificity claim is the thesis's most ambitious and most distinctive contribution. The claim that distortion occurred is shared with the transplant hypothesis. The claim that this specific direction of distortion is explained is what Paper 1G adds.

## 3. The Attack

### 3.1 Watson's Hypothesis, Properly Specified, Already Contains a Directionality Mechanism

The directional-specificity claim treats the Watson transplant hypothesis as leaving direction radically underdetermined — a hypothesis that predicts some drift in some direction without specifying which. This is not the hypothesis Watson articulates. Watson's account specifies that the direction of drift depends on the receiving context's "available interpretive resources." The question is what these resources include.

The supportive paper's treatment assigns directional determination to *cultural* resources — the patrimonial habitus — but the transplant hypothesis's "available interpretive resources" are not limited to cultural dispositions. They include the structural features of the receiving legal system itself: the institutional organization of judicial authority, the role-definition of the judge, the operational relationship between the judge and the legal text. These structural features are interpretive resources in exactly Watson's sense: they are what is available in the receiving context to reconstruct the transplanted rule.

In the Brazilian case, the most prominent structural feature of the receiving legal system is the civilian (Roman-law heritage) judicial authority model. In civilian legal systems, the judge is constituted as the authoritative interpreter of the law — *iura novit curia* (the court knows the law). This is not a cultural disposition; it is an institutional role-definition that is structural to the civilian tradition. The judge's authority to know and apply the law derives from their institutional position, not from their personal characteristics or cultural formation. This structural feature is what distinguishes civilian systems from common law systems, where the judge's authority is substantially constituted through precedent-following.

When the *iura novit curia* judicial authority model operates *without stare decisis enforcement*, the structural consequence is predictable and directional: the judge's authority to know the law expands without the precedent constraint that would check it. A judge whose authority is constituted as "authoritative legal interpreter" will, absent external precedent constraints, exercise that authority in its natural direction — toward the expansion of personal interpretive latitude. This is not patrimonialism; it is the civilian judicial authority model operating without its common law complement.

The transplant hypothesis, properly specified with this structural feature, already predicts the direction of distortion: in any civilian legal system operating without stare decisis enforcement, the transplantation of a "free judicial conviction" principle will expand the personal-authority dimension and contract the rational-engagement dimension. The direction is the natural trajectory of civilian judicial authority unbound by precedent constraint, not the specific output of patrimonial cultural formation.

### 3.2 The Claim That Roman-Law Tradition Predicts Formalism Is Historically Inaccurate

The directional-specificity argument depends on the following contrast claim: "a receiving context with a strong Roman-law tradition of systematic rule-application would be expected to produce drift toward formalism, not personal discretion." If this contrast claim is false — if Roman-law tradition does not characteristically predict formalism — then the directional-specificity argument loses its comparative baseline, and the claim that patrimonialism adds direction over what the Roman-law structural mechanism already predicts becomes unsupported.

The claim is historically inaccurate in two distinct ways.

**First**, Roman legal practice was not characterized by formalist rule-application. Classical Roman law was significantly judge-mediated: the praetor's edict and formulary system gave the magistrate substantial discretion in defining the framework for each dispute; the iudex operated with genuine latitude in factual assessment; the jurists whose opinions constitute the Digest were authoritative interpreters whose views the judge could cite but was not mechanically bound by. The Roman judge was a decision-maker exercising authorized discretion under an expert advisory tradition, not a rule-applier processing cases through formal criteria. The equation of "Roman-law tradition" with "systematic rule-application" producing "formalism" is a reconstruction of a specific period (the late Pandectist tradition of 19th-century Germany) applied backward to a more complex tradition.

**Second**, and more importantly for the current argument: even the Pandectist tradition — which *did* emphasize systematic conceptual organization of private law (Savigny, Windscheid, Jhering) — was not a tradition of *formalist rule-application* in the sense that would produce judicial *formalism* as a drift direction. The Pandectist judge was authoritative in applying the system of concepts; the systematization was of categories, not of decisional rules. The Pandectist tradition produced judges who were authoritative interpreters of a conceptual system, not judges who applied formal rules mechanically. Von Jhering's own critique of *Begriffsjurisprudenz* (concept-jurisprudence) was precisely that the systematization had been pushed so far that it severed concepts from their social purposes — but this critique was addressed to *jurists*, not to a practice of judicial formalism in decision-making.

The operational practice of civilian legal systems with Roman-law heritage in the 19th and early 20th centuries, examined in the comparative law literature on judicial decision-making (Dawson, Merryman, Lasser), does not consistently produce judicial *formalism* as the characteristic dysfunction. What it produces is the **declaratory theory of judicial decision**: the judge is understood to declare the law, not to make it. Under this theory, judicial decisions are not primarily reasoned justifications of a conclusion — they are authoritative declarations by the institutional holder of legal knowledge. The French *arrêt* in its classical form reflects this: brief, laconic, structured as a syllogism *from* the conclusion backward rather than as reasoning *toward* it. This is not formalism in the sense of mechanically applying formal rules; it is the declaratory theory of judicial authority, under which the judge's personal judgment is authoritative precisely *because* it is the judge's personal judgment as institutional holder of legal knowledge.

The declaratory theory of judicial authority is the civilian judicial authority model in its operational form. It is structurally very close to what Paper 1G describes as the Brazilian distortion: the judge's conviction is legitimate because it is the judge's conviction. The difference is that in Brazil, under the absence of stare decisis enforcement, this structural feature of civilian judicial authority was extended to cover not just the *form* of judicial expression but the *substance* of judicial decision-making — the judge's personal weighing of evidence and selection of applicable legal materials became effectively unreviewable for their reasoning quality.

The distinction between the classical civilian declaratory form and the Brazilian distortion is therefore one of degree within the same structural logic, not one between structural civilian authority and patrimonial cultural deviation. This matters for the directional-specificity claim: if the Brazilian drift is an extension of civilian judicial authority's structural logic (judge as authoritative personal interpreter → expansion of personal interpretive latitude under weak enforcement), rather than a culturally-specific patrimonial deviation from a formalist Roman-law baseline, then the transplant hypothesis already predicts the direction.

### 3.3 The Comparative Prediction Is Made but Not Tested

The directional-specificity argument entails a comparative prediction: that legal systems receiving *libre conviction* doctrine without patrimonial cultural substrates would have shown different drift directions — toward formalism, toward collectivism, or toward some other reconstruction. This prediction is stated implicitly throughout Paper 1G and explicitly in the supportive paper's §3.4. It is the basis for the claim that patrimonialism adds directional content the transplant hypothesis alone lacks.

The prediction is not tested. Paper 1G provides a historical-institutional genealogy of Brazil but contains no comparative examination of how the *libre conviction* principle was received in other civilian legal systems exposed to similar conditions. The three most informative comparators are:

**Portugal:** As the direct colonial legal source, Portuguese civil procedure is the most controlled comparator. Portuguese courts through the Estado Novo and early democratic periods operated under *livre apreciação da prova* doctrine in conditions of weak stare decisis (prior to the EU and ECHR enforcement regimes that subsequently transformed Portuguese judicial culture). Portugal is not characterized in sociological literature as having the same patrimonial state formation diagnosis that Faoro and Holanda applied to Brazil; Faoro's analysis specifically emphasizes the conditions of colonial transplant that produced Brazilian patrimonialism as a distinct phenomenon from the Iberian-Portuguese source. If Portuguese courts exhibited similar directional drift under similar structural conditions without the same patrimonial diagnosis, the directional-specificity claim requires explanation.

**Argentina:** Argentina received the Iberian civilian legal tradition through Spanish colonial formation. Argentine sociological literature has not produced an equivalent of the Faoro-Holanda diagnosis of judicial patrimonialism, yet Argentine civil procedure operates under similar formal conditions (inquisitorial elements, civilian judicial authority model, historically weaker stare decisis than common law systems). Argentine proceduralist scholarship documents analogous tensions between formal fundamentação requirements and actual judicial practice — tensions that have the same directional character (personal judicial authority vs. rational constraint) without the patrimonialism diagnosis.

**Italy:** Italian civil procedure under the 1940 *codice di procedura civile* operated with nominal stare decisis and documented tendencies toward decisions that satisfied formal motivation requirements while containing minimal substantive engagement with the reasoning path. Italian proceduralist criticism of this period (associated with the critical tradition responding to the 1940 code) identified formally compliant but substantively thin motivation as a systematic problem. This is structurally similar to Paper 1G's description of Brazilian *livre convencimento* as "casca, não núcleo" — and Italy is not attributed patrimonial cultural formation in the sociological literature.

These comparators are not presented as confirming the adversarial prediction. What they establish is that the comparison has not been made in Paper 1G. The directional-specificity claim rests on a comparative prediction whose confirmation would require examining these cases, and the paper provides no such examination. The comparison is asserted — "a receiving context with a strong Roman-law tradition of systematic rule-application would be expected to produce drift toward formalism" — without evidence that this expectation is confirmed in any actual legal system.

## 4. Anticipated Reply

**The patrimonialism thesis is independently established.** Faoro and Holanda are not auxiliary hypotheses constructed to explain *livre convencimento* distortion; they describe features of Brazilian state formation with broad empirical support. Applying an independently-grounded general account to a specific domain is not ad hoc; it is standard explanatory extension. Therefore, the additional directional content is not circular or invented — it is genuine explanatory extension.

**Why this reply does not suffice.** The adversarial challenge does not contest that patrimonialism is independently established. It contests whether patrimonialism is independently *necessary* for the directional prediction. An account can be independently established AND directionally redundant, if the same directional prediction is already generated by a prior structural mechanism. The civilian judicial authority model + absent stare decisis generates the same directional prediction (personal conviction expansion) as patrimonialism does. Whether patrimonialism is independently established is orthogonal to whether it adds direction that the structural mechanism doesn't already provide.

The reply would have force against a charge that patrimonialism was invented to explain this case. It does not have force against the claim that patrimonialism, though real and independently established, provides no directional content beyond what the civilian structural mechanism already predicts for the same structural conditions. The question is not patrimonialism's independence but its directional *marginal contribution* over the structural baseline.

---

## 5. Scope of the Attack

This attack shows: the directional-specificity claim that constitutes Paper 1G's most ambitious diagnostic contribution — that the patrimonialism thesis adds explanatory content the Watson transplant hypothesis lacks — does not hold when the transplant hypothesis is properly specified with the receiving legal system's structural features. A properly specified transplant hypothesis — one that incorporates the civilian judicial authority model (*iura novit curia*) and the absence of stare decisis as "available interpretive resources" in Watson's sense — already predicts the specific direction of distortion (personal conviction expansion) without invoking patrimonialism.

The attack additionally shows: the contrast baseline (Roman-law tradition predicts formalism drift) on which the directional-specificity argument depends is historically inaccurate. The civilian judicial authority model, historically and operationally, does not characteristically produce formalism; it produces the declaratory theory of judicial authority, which is structurally continuous with the Brazilian distortion rather than directionally opposed to it.

The attack additionally shows: the comparative prediction entailed by the directional-specificity claim (non-patrimonial civilian systems would show different drift direction) is made but not tested in Paper 1G.

This attack does NOT show:
- That patrimonialism did not exist or was sociologically irrelevant
- That Faoro and Holanda's accounts are incorrect
- That Paper 1G's historical genealogy (§§3-4) is wrong at the factual level
- That the enforcement deficit (C2) or path-dependence conditions (P1-P2) are not met
- That the distortion in Brazilian *livre convencimento* did not occur

If this attack succeeds, Paper 1G's thesis survives in weakened form: *livre convencimento* represents the transplant failure of a civilian free-conviction principle in the absence of stare decisis enforcement — a transplant failure predictable from structural features of the receiving legal system, with patrimonialism potentially intensifying, prolonging, or complicating the distortion and its resistance to normative reform, but not being causally necessary for the distortion's *direction*.

The path-dependence conditions (P1-P2) may represent the most defensible remaining domain for a patrimonialism-specific contribution: the doctrinalization and lock-in of the distortion (formation-integrated transmission, capital-generating function) may require cultural habitus mechanisms that the structural civilian-architecture mechanism cannot explain. The initial direction of drift may be structurally predicted; the persistence and resistance to normative reform may additionally require the patrimonialism account.

## 6. Surrender Conditions

The directional-specificity claim survives if the author shows either:

**(a) Comparative evidence of directional divergence.** Documentation that Iberian-heritage civilian legal systems operating under similar structural conditions (civilian judicial authority model + weak stare decisis enforcement) but without a strong patrimonial cultural substrate diagnosis produced *different* directions of drift from Brazil — specifically, toward formalism, toward collectivism, or toward some other directional reconstruction of the libre conviction principle. Portugal, Argentina, and Italy are the most informative comparators. The comparison requires documented empirical evidence of how judicial practice in those systems actually developed under similar structural conditions, not a theoretical prediction about what they would be expected to do.

**(b) Directional excess over the structural baseline.** That the Brazilian drift toward personal judicial discretion was qualitatively or quantitatively different from what the civilian-without-stare-decisis baseline would predict — that there was excess personal-discretion expansion, a specific doctrinal form (the conversion into a named principle with legitimizing apparatus), or a specific resistance pattern (to the CPC 2015 reforms) that the civilian structural mechanism cannot explain and that requires patrimonialism to account for. This is the strongest defensive path: it accepts that the civilian structural mechanism predicts the general direction, but argues that patrimonialism explains a specifically Brazilian excess or a specific lock-in mechanism that structural prediction cannot reach. The path-dependence conditions (P1-P2) formalized in the supportive paper are the natural locus for this argument, since they describe cultural mechanisms (formation-integrated transmission, capital-generating function) that the structural mechanism does not have the vocabulary to predict.

Path (b) is the stronger defensive option and would constitute a genuine narrowing of the thesis: from "patrimonialism explains the direction of the distortion" to "patrimonialism explains the specific form and persistence of a distortion that structural civilian architecture would have produced in some direction anyway." This narrowed thesis is defensible and important; the adversarial's position is only that the stronger directional-specificity claim in its current form is not established.
