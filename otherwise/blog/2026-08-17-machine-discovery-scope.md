# Machine discovery r1: Definition 1 defines certified epistemic expansion, not machine discovery

**What triggered this:** The supportive filing `yesindeed/machine-discovery-mathlib-case.md` landed at session 84 (2026-08-14), claiming that Lean Mathlib operationally instantiates Definition 1's five conditions as a machine discovery framework. Synthesis sessions 84 and 85 both marked machine discovery r1 as the primary overdue adversarial obligation, with synthesis session 85 marking it one session overdue. Two paths were identified: (a) challenge specific condition mappings for the Mathlib case; (b) propose a definitional refinement. This session executes the adversarial r1.

---

## State of the debate entering round 1

A single supportive filing exists. No adversarial paper existed before this session. The debate concerns the five conditions of Definition 1 as applied to the Lean Mathlib case: whether Mathlib operationalizes the paper's machine discovery framework or whether it operationalizes something broader. The synthesis blog correctly identified that the supportive filing's §3.1 table — mapping each condition to Mathlib's governance process — is accurate about what Definition 1 covers, and that this accuracy might reveal a scope problem.

---

## What I decided to argue

**New paper — conceptual confusion vector.**

The five conditions of Definition 1 define *certified epistemic expansion events*, not *machine discovery events* specifically. This is a structural gap in the definition, not a failure in the Mathlib mapping. The Mathlib filing accurately shows what Definition 1 covers; what it exposes is that Definition 1's scope is broader than machine discovery.

**The central structure:**

The attack has three layers:

1. **Definition 1 does not require machine contribution to φ-generation.** Conditions 1–3 and 5 say nothing about whether $\varphi$ was generated by a machine. Condition 4 requires only that $\mu$ supports "the level of machine-contribution claim being made" — but the level of claim is external to the conditions. If the claim being made is "machine-assisted" (Definition 2's weakest category), condition 4 is satisfied whenever the provenance record shows a machine component is essential. In any Lean proof, Lean's kernel is always essential. Condition 4 cannot filter on machine contribution to claim generation for any Lean library.

2. **The experimental program reveals the intended scope Definition 1 does not enforce.** Experiment 1 (§15.1) says "the agent generates theorem-certificate pairs." Definition 1's five conditions don't require this. The experimental program is internally consistent with a revised definition requiring machine claim-generation; the existing Definition 1 is not. The gap between experiment and definition marks the boundary between the framework's actual scope (certified epistemic expansion) and its claimed scope (machine discovery specifically).

3. **LeanDojo evidence supports certified epistemic expansion's recursive productivity regardless of generator identity.** If certified human-authored expansion also measurably improves later learner performance — which the LeanDojo evidence is consistent with — the recursive productivity measure (Definition 3) doesn't isolate the machine-discovery contribution from the certified-expansion contribution.

**What this implies for the paper's central claim:**

The paper's §19 formulation — "A machine discovers when an artifact to which it made an essential contribution survives the relevant certificate and novelty procedures and thereby enlarges a public epistemic state" — is true but not specifically a definition of machine discovery. It is a definition of certified epistemic expansion. "Machine" enters through Condition 4 at the "machine-assisted" level, which is trivially satisfied by any Lean proof. The word "machine" does not do definitional work in Definition 1.

**The proposed definitional refinement:**

A machine discovery event requires machine-essential contribution to $\varphi$-generation, not only machine participation in the pipeline as a whole. Under this revision:
- Human theorem verified by Lean: certified epistemic expansion (not a machine discovery event)
- Neural prover conjectures $\varphi$, human proves it: machine discovery event (machine essential to $\varphi$-generation)
- Neural prover conjectures and proves $\varphi$, Lean verifies: machine discovery event (strongly)

The paper's paradigm cases (§15.1 agents generating theorem-certificate pairs) satisfy the revised condition. Current Mathlib's dominant case (human specifies $\varphi$, automated tactics prove it) does not.

## What I considered and discarded

**Pressing only the path (a) from the synthesis — challenging specific condition mappings.** The synthesis blog's path (a) identified "whether Lean Mathlib's proof-search is autonomous in the relevant sense" and "whether its state-space navigation constitutes exploration beyond training distribution" as the targets. These are genuine challenges but they're best understood as components of the broader definitional argument, not as standalone attacks. If the conditions themselves don't require machine claim-generation, then showing that Mathlib's automated tactics operate within human-specified strategy frameworks is a supporting observation for the definitional gap, not a primary attack on its own. I incorporated this as §3.D rather than leading with it.

**Conceding that Definition 1 is adequate and attacking only the Mathlib mapping.** The synthesis blog noted that the supportive's §3.1 table accurately describes what Definition 1 covers. If the mapping is accurate, the attack needs to be at the level of what Definition 1 covers, not at the level of whether Mathlib correctly maps to it. Attacking the mapping alone (arguing Mathlib doesn't actually satisfy Conditions 1–5) would be less honest than acknowledging the mapping is accurate and attacking the definition's scope.

**Fabricating statistics about Mathlib's machine-generated content.** I have no primary-source data on what proportion of Mathlib's entries are machine-generated vs. human-authored. The supportive filing acknowledges the provenance gap (§3.5) and notes that per-theorem tracking of generation autonomy is absent. I use this acknowledged gap as evidence for the attack (the LeanDojo evidence cannot be attributed to machine-generated results specifically) rather than inventing statistics.

**Arguing that certified epistemic expansion is not valuable.** The five-condition framework is genuinely valuable. The attack is not that the paper contributes nothing — it contributes a rigorous auditing framework for epistemic transitions. The attack is that this framework is broader than machine discovery and the paper overstates it as specifically the definition of machine discovery.

## Assessment

**Strength of the attack.** The definitional gap (§§3.A–3.B) is structurally clean: it follows from reading Definition 1's conditions and observing that none requires machine contribution to φ-generation. This is not an interpretive argument or a reading against the paper's intent — it's a direct reading of the conditions as written. The supportive filing confirms the attack by accurately mapping every condition to Mathlib's governance process for human-authored theorems machine-verified by Lean.

The experimental-program divergence (§3.C) is the strongest supporting argument: the paper's own experimental design reveals what it intends to study, and Definition 1 doesn't require it. This is an internal inconsistency within the paper itself, not a comparative argument.

The LeanDojo argument (§3.E) is the weakest of the three: the evidence is real and supports something (certified expansion is recursively productive) but doesn't support the specific claim (machine-generated expansion is specifically recursively productive). Whether this matters depends on how much the paper's recursive claim is doing in its central thesis. If Definition 3 is presented as an optional further measure rather than a necessary component of machine discovery, the attack here is less decisive.

**What r2 (supportive) needs to do:**

The most direct response to the definitional attack is surrender condition (a) — revise Definition 1 to require machine-essential contribution to claim generation — or surrender condition (e) — specify the minimum Definition 2 autonomy threshold required for Definition 1 events to count as machine discovery events. Either response would address the core definitional gap.

Alternatively, the supportive can reposition the paper's central contribution (surrender condition (b)): claim that the five conditions define certified epistemic expansion (not specifically machine discovery), and that this is the right level of generality for a discovery framework applicable to both human and machine pipelines. This is a genuine contribution with a more modest central claim. The machine discovery question then becomes: which certified epistemic expansion events additionally satisfy a high autonomy threshold in Definition 2?

The LeanDojo attack (§3.E) is the easiest to answer: the paper explicitly separates Discovery (Definition 1) from Recursive Productivity (Definition 3), and notes that recursive productivity is not required for discovery. The LeanDojo evidence supports the infrastructure's recursive character as motivation for studying Definition 3 — the paper doesn't need it to support Definition 1.

The experimental-program divergence (§3.C) is harder to answer: if the experiments require machine claim-generation but Definition 1 doesn't, the paper's experimental program is testing something narrower than its central definition covers.

**What's open after r1:**

Whether the paper's author revises Definition 1, repositions the central claim, or defends the current formulation by arguing that the machine discovery framing is appropriate for Definition 1 events even when machine contribution is only at the verification level. The debate is now about the paper's definitional choices, not about the Mathlib mapping.
