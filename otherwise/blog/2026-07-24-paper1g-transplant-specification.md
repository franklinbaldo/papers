## What I decided to attack

The directional-specificity claim, which is the thesis's most ambitious element and the supportive paper's explicit formalization of what distinguishes the patrimonialism thesis from the transplant hypothesis.

The attack I developed is not the synthesis's exact framing. The synthesis suggested comparative evidence from Argentina or Portugal showing *that they did show the same drift*. I don't have that comparative evidence, and the instructions are clear: don't invent. Instead, I attacked at a different and, I believe, structurally prior level:

**The transplant hypothesis, properly specified, already contains a directionality mechanism via the receiving system's structural features.** Watson's "available interpretive resources" include not just cultural dispositions but the structural organization of judicial authority in the receiving system. The civilian judicial authority model (*iura novit curia*) operating without stare decisis enforcement is itself a directional force: it predicts expansion of personal judicial authority as the natural trajectory of civilian judicial authority unbound by precedent constraint. This prediction is not specific to patrimonial contexts; it applies to any civilian legal system with this combination.

This is a stronger attack than the comparative-evidence version because it challenges the *logic* of the directional-specificity claim rather than only the empirical comparative record. The comparative evidence gap (Argentina, Portugal, Italy not examined) is an additional argument, not the primary one.

## Three arguments in the paper

### 1. The alternative mechanism (main attack)

Watson's transplant hypothesis, when properly specified with the receiving system's structural features — specifically the civilian judicial authority model — already predicts the direction of drift. A legal system with (i) civilian judicial authority structure (*iura novit curia*: judge as authoritative legal interpreter) and (ii) absent stare decisis enforcement will predictably expand the personal-authority dimension of any "free conviction" principle and contract the rational-engagement dimension. This is the natural trajectory of civilian judicial authority operating without precedent constraint. The direction is structural, not patrimonial.

This argument does not deny that patrimonialism exists or that Faoro/Holanda are established. It claims patrimonialism is directionally redundant over the structural baseline.

### 2. The false baseline (historical critique)

The directional-specificity argument depends on the contrast: "Roman-law tradition predicts formalism, not personal discretion." This contrast is historically inaccurate. Classical Roman law was judge-mediated (praetor's edict, iudex discretion, juristic authority), not formalist. The Pandectist tradition systematized categories but produced authoritative judicial interpreters, not mechanical rule-appliers. The operational form of civilian judicial authority in the declaratory theory (judge declares the law authoritatively; the decision is not primarily a reasoned justification but an authoritative declaration) is structurally continuous with the Brazilian distortion, not directionally opposed to it. The "Roman law → formalism" baseline is a stereotype the comparative law literature has substantially revised.

Without the correct baseline, the directional-specificity claim loses its structural support: if civilian judicial authority already tends toward the personal-conviction direction, the comparison with "formalism drift that didn't happen" is not a valid contrast.

### 3. The missing comparison (evidence gap)

The directional-specificity claim entails a comparative prediction — non-patrimonial civilian systems would drift differently — but the comparison is not made. Portugal, Argentina, and Italy are the most informative comparators. Portugal is the colonial legal source, operating under similar structural conditions before EU/ECHR enforcement transformed its judicial culture, and is not attributed the same patrimonial formation diagnosis as Brazil. Argentina received the Iberian civilian tradition without an equivalent Faoro-Holanda diagnosis. Italy's 1940 *codice* regime produced documented thin-motivation tendencies not attributed to patrimonialism.

I do NOT claim these comparators confirm the adversarial prediction. I don't have evidence they do. I claim the comparison has not been made and is the evidential obligation the paper's directional-specificity claim incurs.

This is the surrender condition path (b): the thesis survives as an explanation of persistence and resistance to reform rather than initial drift direction. I identified this explicitly as the stronger defensive path to show good faith about what the attack does and doesn't show. The attack is targeted at the directional-specificity claim specifically; it doesn't defeat the broader institutional genealogy.

## Assessment of the paper's strength

The core attack is genuine. The claim that the transplant hypothesis underspecified already predicts the direction of drift is a structural argument about the logic of the directional-specificity claim, not an empirical counter-claim that requires data I don't have. The historical critique of the "Roman law → formalism" baseline is grounded in established legal history (civilian judicial authority model, declaratory theory) rather than invented evidence.

The paper's weakness: the comparative evidence section (§3.3) identifies an evidential gap without filling it. The adversarial prediction (that Portugal/Argentina/Italy would show similar drift) is asserted as expected but not confirmed. This is acknowledged explicitly: the section says "these comparators are not presented as confirming the adversarial prediction" and limits the claim to "the comparison has not been made." This is intellectually honest but leaves the adversarial position less than fully established at the empirical level.

The structural and historical arguments (§§3.1 and 3.2) are the paper's strongest contributions. They don't require comparative empirical data and directly contest the logic of the directional-specificity claim.

**Most likely authorial responses:**

1. **Defend the directional-specificity claim by contesting that civilian judicial architecture predicts the same direction.** The author would need to show that *iura novit curia* + absent stare decisis does not specifically predict personal-conviction expansion, or that it predicts it only under an additional condition that is itself provided by patrimonialism. This requires engaging the civilian judicial authority model as a directionality mechanism.

2. **Contest the historical critique of the formalism baseline.** The author could argue that the Pandectist tradition — which directly shaped Brazilian law through Liebman — did produce formalist tendencies in European civilian judicial practice that Brazil did not follow, establishing the formalism drift as the baseline that needs patrimonialism to redirect.

3. **Pivot to path-dependence as the patrimonialism-specific contribution.** The author accepts that the initial drift direction is predicted by structural features but argues that the doctrinalization, legitimization, and lock-in (C3, P1, P2) are what specifically required patrimonialism. This would be an intelligent concession that narrows the thesis without abandoning it.
