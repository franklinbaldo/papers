# Certified Epistemic Expansion Without Machine Discovery: Definition 1 Does Not Distinguish Machine-Originated from Human-Authored Certified Discovery

---

## 1. Thesis Attacked

The target paper `machine_discovery.md` — "When the Learner Changes the Curriculum" (Baldo, 2026) — presents Definition 1 as the formal definition of a machine discovery event:

> "The transition $K_t \xrightarrow{a} K_{t+1}$ is an **accepted discovery event** relative to $(K_t, \equiv_t, \mathcal{A}_t, B_N)$ when: (1) Admissibility... (2) Certification... (3) Snapshot novelty... (4) Provenance sufficiency: $\mu$ supports the level of machine-contribution claim being made. (5) Public uptake..." (§9, Definition 1)

And its central summary (§19):

> "A machine discovers when an artifact to which it made an essential contribution survives the relevant certificate and novelty procedures and thereby enlarges a public epistemic state."

The supportive filing "Formal Library Growth as Certified Epistemic Expansion: The Lean Mathlib Case" (`yesindeed/machine-discovery-mathlib-case.md`) claims:

> "The five-condition framework (Definition 1) is operationally instantiated in the Lean Mathlib ecosystem... Mathlib is not a candidate ledger but a certified epistemic state in the paper's sense: every accepted entry satisfies all five conditions relative to the prior library state as the frozen snapshot."

This paper attacks a premise shared by the framework paper's Definition 1, the supportive Mathlib instantiation, and the direct Definition 1 defense (`yesindeed/definition1-machine-discovery-defense.md`). The Lean Mathlib case correctly shows that Definition 1 is operationally instantiable. In doing so, it exposes that Definition 1 defines **certified epistemic expansion events** — not **machine discovery events**. The conceptual distinction the paper promises to clarify — between human discovery machine-verified and machine discovery properly — is not built into Definition 1. It is delegated to a post-hoc classification in Definition 2, with no minimum threshold specified in Definition 1 itself and no minimum threshold specified for Definition 2 either. The §19 central-claim formulation is, as a consequence, satisfied by pipelines in which no machine contributes to generating the artifact.

---

## 2. Faithful Reconstruction

The paper's strongest contribution is the certified-transition framework: a discovery event should be understood as a change in a public epistemic state — a state with six explicitly specified components (language, inference rules, accepted claims, certificates, provenance records, audit policy) — not as a model output evaluated in isolation. The paper correctly decomposes discovery into six separable questions (§3): is the claim well-formed? certified? new? properly attributed? incorporated? does it change what later learners can do? This decomposition is valuable independent of whether the generator is human or machine.

The paper's explicit refusal to conflate novelty, certification, and attribution is the clearest advance over informal discussions that treat impressiveness of output as a proxy for all of these. The five conditions specify what is needed for an auditable epistemic transition. The autonomy vector (Definition 2) and the recursive productivity measure (Definition 3) specify what to study within that transition. The experimental program (§15) and the falsifiable hypotheses (§16) connect the framework to tractable empirical tests.

The paper is also explicit that discovery may range over more than propositions. §5 states the candidate artifact carries a claim $\varphi$ that may be "the proposed claim, construction, algorithm, or empirical regularity." §8.3 characterizes the object of a machine-generation contribution as "the novel claim, construction, or method." Definition 2 speaks of machine essentiality "to the first generation of the novel claim, construction, or method." The paper's φ is deliberately broader than "theorem statement." This is a strength of the framework and is preserved throughout the analysis below.

The strongest version of the paper's claim is: discovery — whether by machines or humans — should be tracked through the certified epistemic transition framework, and machine contributions should be scored against an autonomy vector rather than a binary label. The paper correctly notes that novelty, correctness, provenance, and attribution are different properties requiring different evidence. These are genuine advances.

The supportive Mathlib filing correctly identifies that this framework is already instantiated in a major formal library ecosystem. The audit boundary (PR + kernel verification), the versioned epistemic state (Mathlib main branch), the provenance record (Git history), and the acceptance policy (CI + small-kernel verification + reviewer novelty check) correspond to the paper's framework components. Lean Mathlib is the paper's "cleanest initial test bed" already in operation as a certified epistemic expansion system. The supportive filing is accurate about this.

The direct Definition 1 defense adds a further reconstruction worth reproducing at its strongest: Definition 1 is deliberately generator-agnostic because the epistemic requirements for a certified transition should not depend on generator identity, and Definition 2 is deliberately the classification stage because generator identity is a graded property (the autonomy vector) rather than a binary one. On this reading, the auditing/classification separation is the framework's principal architectural decision, not an oversight. This is the strongest reconstruction of the framework's design intent and is treated as authoritative for the analysis below.

## 3. The Attack

### 3.A Definition 1 Does Not Require Machine Contribution to Claim Generation

Definition 1's five conditions test four properties of the artifact $a = (\varphi, \pi, \mu, \sigma)$ and one property of the provenance trace $\mu$:

1. Whether $\varphi$ is well-formed in the accepted language (**admissibility**)
2. Whether $\pi$ certifies $\varphi$ under the accepted policy (**certification**)
3. Whether $\varphi$ has no equivalent prior accepted claim (**snapshot novelty**)
4. Whether $\mu$ supports the machine-contribution claim being made (**provenance sufficiency**)
5. Whether $a$ is incorporated into the public state (**public uptake**)

None of these five conditions requires that $\varphi$ was *generated* by a machine, or that any novel step in $\pi$ was generated by a machine, or that any machine contribution was essential to producing $a$ as opposed to only verifying it. Condition 4 requires only that the provenance record is adequate for the level of machine-contribution claim being made — but the level of claim being made is external to the five conditions. The conditions specify what documentation is required *given* a claim; they do not specify what claim must be made or what minimum contribution the claim must assert.

The result is that Definition 1 is satisfied by any certified epistemic expansion event with a machine anywhere in the pipeline, regardless of whether the machine contributed to generating $\varphi$ or to any novel structural step in $\pi$. A library of entirely human-generated and human-proved theorems, verified by Lean's kernel, satisfies all five conditions:

- **Admissibility**: human-written Lean statements are well-formed in Lean's dependent type-theoretic language. ✓  
- **Certification**: Lean's kernel verifies that human-written proof terms inhabit the claimed types. ✓  
- **Snapshot novelty**: Mathlib's PR review process checks for equivalent existing results. ✓  
- **Provenance sufficiency**: Git records the human contributor; the machine-contribution claim made is "machine-assisted" (Lean's kernel is essential). The provenance supports this claim. ✓  
- **Public uptake**: merged into Mathlib's main branch with a stable, versioned API name. ✓

The supportive filing's §3.1 table confirms this precisely. Its description of every condition maps to a governance process designed for human-authored theorems machine-verified by Lean. The table does not describe, and Mathlib's governance does not require, machine contribution to the generation of the theorem statement $\varphi$ or to any novel structural step in the proof term $\pi$. The table is an accurate description of what Definition 1 covers. That accuracy is the problem.

### 3.B Condition 4 Is Trivially Satisfied for Any Lean Proof at the Machine-Assisted Level, and §19 Is False as Stated

Condition 4's modularity — "μ supports the level of machine-contribution claim being made" — creates an asymmetric filtering structure. The condition cannot fail for any claim level that the provenance adequately supports. If the claim being made is "machine-assisted" (Definition 2's weakest category: at least one machine component on an essential path in $\mu$), then Condition 4 is satisfied whenever the provenance record shows a machine component is essential.

In Lean Mathlib, Lean's kernel is always essential: no proof enters Mathlib without kernel acceptance. Removing the kernel from the pipeline prevents any proof from being accepted. Therefore, Lean's kernel is always an essential machine component. Therefore, every Mathlib proof trivially satisfies "machine-assisted" with adequate provenance support.

Condition 4 as applied to any Lean library cannot filter theorems on machine contribution to generation. For any human-authored theorem in Mathlib, the provenance record supports the "machine-assisted" claim (Lean verified it), and Condition 4 is satisfied. The condition is vacuous in this context: it cannot distinguish between a theorem whose claim $\varphi$ was generated by a human and one generated by a machine, as long as the provenance record is adequate for the respective claim levels.

The §19 central-claim formulation — "A machine discovers when an artifact to which it made an essential contribution survives the relevant certificate and novelty procedures and thereby enlarges a public epistemic state" — is a definition by satisfaction: a machine discovers when Definition 1 is satisfied and the machine's contribution to the artifact is essential. In the Mathlib human-authored-theorem case above, Definition 1 is satisfied (all five conditions), and a machine contribution — kernel verification — is essential to the artifact (removing the kernel prevents acceptance). Under the §19 formulation as stated, this counts as machine discovery. It obviously should not. The problem is not that §19's word "essential" is being read incorrectly; it is that Definition 1 provides no criterion for *which kind* of essential contribution is required, and any essential contribution suffices under the §19 formulation.

A framework designed specifically to track machine discovery must either (a) specify a minimum kind of machine contribution in Definition 1 itself (e.g., contribution to generating $\varphi$ or to the novel structural step in $\pi$, not only to verifying a human-specified $\varphi$), or (b) specify a minimum level of the Definition 2 autonomy vector such that "machine discovery event" is coextensive with "Definition 1 event above that threshold." The paper does neither. The category "machine discovery event" is therefore not defined by the framework as it stands.

### 3.C The Experimental Program Reveals the Intended Scope That Definition 1 Does Not Enforce

Experiment 1 (§15.1) describes the cleanest test of the framework:

> "Freeze a proof-assistant library at commit $K_t$. Permit the discovery agent access only to: the frozen axioms and theorems; documented tactics; a declared compute budget; and no later commits. The agent generates theorem-certificate pairs."

The agent in this experiment *generates* $\varphi$ — the novel claim, in the strongest possible sense: statement plus certificate, from a frozen library, with no human intervention post-freeze. This is the activity the paper's title and thesis are about: a system producing the claim, not merely verifying it. Definition 1 does not enforce this requirement. Its five conditions are satisfiable whether $\varphi$ was generated by the agent or by a human mathematician who opened a pull request.

The paper's experimental program is internally consistent with a revised definition requiring machine claim-generation (in the paper's own broad sense: novel claim, construction, or method). The existing Definition 1 is not. The five conditions specify the auditing requirements for a certified epistemic transition; the experimental design specifies the activity the paper's central thesis concerns. These are different specifications. Their divergence marks the boundary between the framework's actual scope (certified epistemic expansion) and its claimed scope (machine discovery specifically).

This gap between experimental design and formal definition is not incidental. The paper's §2 explicitly states it does not claim "that one spectacular output establishes a general capacity for autonomous research." This restraint presupposes that "autonomous research" is the phenomenon in view: a machine generating the research independently, not just verifying human-specified work. Definition 1 as written does not enforce autonomy of research generation as a necessary condition for a discovery event to occur.

### 3.D Mathlib's Automated Components Operate Within Human-Specified Strategy Frameworks

The supportive Mathlib filing (§4, anticipated objection) acknowledges that "the autonomy vectors for most Mathlib entries would have near-zero machine-generation components." The machine components that do appear in Mathlib proof pipelines operate within strategy frameworks specified by humans:

- **`aesop`**: applies a human-designed rewriting strategy to a human-specified goal;
- **`decide`**: exhausts a computationally bounded search space whose bounds, admissibility conditions, and problem class were human-specified by the tactic implementers;
- **`polyrith`**: calls an external algebraic system for a problem class identified by the human tactic authors;
- **`norm_num`**: normalizes numerical expressions according to human-designed reduction rules.

These automated components reduce proof effort for problems in their domains. They do not generate the theorem-claim $\varphi$ and they do not introduce novel structural steps in $\pi$ — they instantiate human-designed strategies to close human-specified goals. In each case, a human mathematician specifies $\varphi$ in a Lean pull request; the tactic finds a proof term $\pi$ for the human-specified $\varphi$ by automated search within a bounded space whose admissibility structure is fixed by the tactic's human authors. The machine's essential contribution is to proof search given a human-specified problem and a human-specified strategy space — not to the generation of the problem itself or to the identification of novel structural steps beyond the strategy framework.

Condition 4 is satisfied for the "machine-assisted" claim in these cases: the automated tactic is essential (without it, the proof term may not close within reasonable effort). But the machine's contribution to $\varphi$-generation is zero and its contribution to the novel structural step in $\pi$ is zero (the "structural step" is the human-designed strategy the tactic instantiates, not something the tactic itself discovered). A machine discovery framework should distinguish between machine-essential contribution to the generation of $\varphi$ (or to identifying novel structural steps in $\pi$) and machine-essential contribution to the automated instantiation of a human-specified strategy for a human-specified $\varphi$. Definition 1 makes this distinction only through Definition 2's autonomy vector — which is a classification tool applied after Definition 1 is satisfied, not a condition of Definition 1 itself, and which the paper does not specify a threshold for.

### 3.E The LeanDojo Recursive Productivity Evidence Supports Certified Expansion, Not Machine Discovery Specifically

The supportive filing (§3.3) cites Yang et al. (2023) as evidence for recursive productivity (Definition 3): neural provers with retrieval access to larger Mathlib libraries substantially outperform provers with access to smaller libraries, on tasks requiring library lemmas as building blocks.

This evidence supports the claim: *certified epistemic expansion is recursively productive for later learners* — larger certified libraries improve subsequent proof-search. This is genuine and the LeanDojo evidence supports it.

What the evidence does not support is the claim that *machine-generated* certified results specifically produce the recursive benefit, as distinct from *human-generated* certified results of equivalent size. The Mathlib entries improving LeanDojo performance at present are, as the supportive acknowledges, predominantly human-authored. The LeanDojo evidence therefore supports the general recursive productivity of certified epistemic expansion — regardless of generator identity — rather than the machine-discovery-specific recursive consequence the paper's §19 formulates.

The paper's recursive claim requires more precision: if certified human-authored expansion also measurably improves what later learners can infer (which the LeanDojo evidence establishes), the recursive productivity measure (Definition 3) does not isolate the machine discovery contribution from the certified expansion contribution. The hypothesis worth testing — that machine-originated certified results produce recursive benefits over and above what human-generated certified results of equivalent epistemic character achieve — is not tested by the LeanDojo evidence. Using LeanDojo as evidence for recursive machine discovery conflates the established infrastructure for certified epistemic expansion with the specific claim about machine-originated expansion's recursive contribution.

### 3.F The Proposed Refinement Does Not Exclude Proof-Level Machine Contributions

The natural framework-preserving defense of Definition 1's silence on machine-contribution is: any refinement that added machine-φ-generation as a Definition 1 condition would exclude the paradigm cases the paper cites — AlphaGeometry, AlphaTensor, FunSearch, Minimo — because these systems make machine contributions primarily at the proof or construction level rather than at the theorem-statement level. This section shows the defense fails: it depends on reading $\varphi$ narrowly as "theorem statement," when the paper's own text ranges $\varphi$ over "claim, construction, algorithm, or empirical regularity" (§5) and Definition 2's own text ranges the object of machine-generation over "the novel claim, construction, or method."

The proposed refinement, stated in the paper's own vocabulary, is: **Definition 1 requires machine-essential contribution to the first generation of the novel claim, construction, or method $\varphi$, or to identifying the novel structural step in the certificate $\pi$, rather than only to automated instantiation of a human-specified strategy for a human-specified $\varphi$ or to verification-level participation.** With this refinement:

- **AlphaGeometry** (Trinh et al., 2024, §3 of the target paper): the system discovers auxiliary geometric constructions that are essential to closing the proof and that are not fixed points of any human-designed strategy space — the auxiliary construction is a novel construction, hence a novel $\varphi$ under §5's broader reading, and the machine's contribution to its generation is essential. AlphaGeometry satisfies the refined Definition 1.

- **AlphaTensor** (Fawzi et al., 2022, §3): the system discovers matrix-multiplication algorithms improving known bounds. The algorithm *is* the novel construction or method — the object of the discovery claim. The human specification is the problem class ("find a faster algorithm for $n \times n$"); the algorithm itself is machine-generated. Under §8.3's language, this is essential machine contribution to "the first generation of the novel method." AlphaTensor satisfies the refined Definition 1.

- **FunSearch** (§3): the system produces new constructions and algorithms for established open problems. The construction is the novel object; the machine's contribution to its generation is essential. FunSearch satisfies the refined Definition 1.

- **Minimo** (§3): jointly generates conjectures and searches for proofs. Conjecture-generation is machine-essential to the generation of the novel claim. Minimo satisfies the refined Definition 1.

The refinement excludes the case the framework should exclude: a human-authored Mathlib theorem where the machine contributions are Lean's kernel (verification) and automated tactics operating within human-designed strategy spaces (instantiation of human strategy, not generation of novel structural step). It preserves the paradigm cases the framework is designed to capture.

The framework-preserving defense of the current Definition 1 therefore rests on a mischaracterization of what the refinement requires. The defense treats the refinement as "machine must generate the theorem statement" — a strictly narrower condition than the paper's own §8.3 language authorizes. Once the refinement is stated in the paper's own vocabulary, the paradigm-case-exclusion argument does not go through, and the framework-preserving defense loses its principal move.

The refinement is not the only formulation available: surrender condition (e) — specifying a minimum Definition 2 autonomy threshold without revising Definition 1 — is equally compatible with the framework and easier to install. But the refinement's availability closes off the strongest framework-preserving defense of leaving Definition 1 as it stands.

## 4. Anticipated Replies

**Reply to 3.A–3.B: "The auditing/classification separation is the framework's principal architectural decision. Definition 1 is generator-agnostic by design; Definition 2 provides the machine/human classification. Combining them would make generator identity a condition of epistemic acceptance and would defeat a design goal — auditors should be able to evaluate epistemic status without needing to determine generator identity first. The paper's Definition 2 explicitly distinguishes machine-assisted from machine-originated. This is not a gap; it is the framework."**

*Why this does not suffice.* The reply is the strongest form of the framework-preserving move and its architectural motivation is genuine. It nevertheless does not defeat the attack — it concedes the attack's premise while attempting to reposition the paper's central claim.

Grant the reply's architectural point: Definition 1 is generator-agnostic on purpose. The attack becomes: if Definition 1 is generator-agnostic on purpose, then the paper's central §19 formulation — "A machine discovers when an artifact to which it made an essential contribution survives the relevant certificate and novelty procedures and thereby enlarges a public epistemic state" — is not a definition of machine discovery. It is a definition of certified epistemic expansion satisfied by any pipeline containing at least one essential machine component (which the paper stipulates as a precondition of Definition 1 at §9). In the human-authored, kernel-verified Mathlib case, Lean's kernel is an essential machine component of the artifact, Definition 1 is satisfied, and §19's conditions are met. The formulation gives the wrong answer for a paradigm case it should exclude.

The reply's architectural move therefore requires one of two corollary commitments:

(i) **Rewrite §19 (and the framework's abstract-level claims) to say what the architecture actually supports.** Under the auditing/classification separation, the correct central formulation is: "A machine discovers when Definition 1 is satisfied *and* the Definition 2 autonomy vector reaches a specified minimum threshold." The framework's presentation currently states the former without the latter. Rewriting the central claim to match the architecture converts the framework into an explicit certified-epistemic-expansion framework plus a classifier — a genuine contribution more precisely scoped than the current claim, and one the attack has never contested. This is surrender condition (b) as originally stated; the auditing/classification separation defense is best read as an argument *for* this reformulation, not against the attack.

(ii) **Or specify the Definition 2 threshold.** The auditing/classification separation defense presupposes that Definition 2 does the machine-discovery-specific work Definition 1 does not. For Definition 2 to do that work, the paper must specify what level of the autonomy vector counts as machine discovery. It does not. The direct defense (§4 of `yesindeed/definition1-machine-discovery-defense.md`) explicitly declines to argue for specifying this threshold ("this would be a clarification and would strengthen the framework... but I don't argue for this change — that's for the author to consider"). Declining to argue for the threshold specification is the concession, not the rebuttal, of surrender condition (e). Without the threshold, Definition 2 cannot do the machine-discovery-specific work the architectural separation defense assigns to it.

The reply thus splits the attack's two structural claims: it defeats the reading that the auditing function is architecturally misplaced (which the attack never made) and concedes the reading that the paper's central formulation is not underwritten by the framework as stated (which is the attack). What the reply's authors intend as a rebuttal is best read as an argument for surrender condition (b) or (e), depending on whether the paper's central claims are revised or the threshold is specified.

The distinction is not terminological. It determines what the Mathlib evidence establishes. The Mathlib filing claims it "operationalizes Definition 1 in the paper's sense" as evidence that the machine discovery framework is instantiated. If Definition 1 operationalizes certified epistemic expansion (regardless of machine discovery, per the auditing/classification separation), then the Mathlib evidence establishes the framework's operational coherence as an epistemic-expansion tracker — not that machine discovery is occurring at scale in Mathlib. These are different claims, and the difference matters for how the paper's research agenda is scoped.

**Reply to 3.B: "Condition 4's 'trivially satisfied at the lowest autonomy level' is the correct behavior for a graded framework, not a defect. The alternative — requiring Condition 4 to be non-trivially satisfied — would mean that human-authored theorems kernel-verified by Lean fail Definition 1 entirely, narrowing the framework's applicability to only the most autonomous cases and excluding the intermediate cases that make up the bulk of contemporary human-machine collaborative discovery."**

*Why this does not suffice.* The reply misidentifies what the trivial-satisfaction observation is meant to establish. The attack does not require that human-authored kernel-verified theorems fail Condition 4. It requires that the framework specify a floor below which an event that satisfies Definition 1 does not count as a "machine discovery event" as distinct from a "certified epistemic expansion event." The floor may be located inside Condition 4 (revising the condition), in a new Condition 6 (adding a machine-contribution requirement), or in a specified Definition 2 threshold (leaving Definition 1 unchanged). All three locations preserve the framework's coverage of intermediate cases — they specify *where the intermediate cases begin*, which the framework currently does not.

The reply's dilemma — either non-triviality (which excludes the intermediates) or trivial satisfiability (which is the current framework) — is a false dichotomy. A specified threshold at Definition 2 preserves intermediate case coverage while giving the category "machine discovery event" a floor. The reply argues persuasively against the non-triviality horn but does not address the threshold horn, and the attack rests on the threshold horn. The direct defense's own §4 concedes the threshold horn's tractability: it acknowledges that specifying a minimum Definition 2 threshold "would be a clarification and would strengthen the framework." It declines to argue for the specification. Declining to argue is not rebutting.

**Reply to 3.C–3.D–3.F: "Requiring machine φ-generation in Definition 1 would exclude AlphaGeometry (proof-level contribution), AlphaTensor (algorithm discovery for human-specified problem class), and similar paradigm cases where machine contribution is primarily to π rather than to φ. The autonomy vector's multidimensionality — high α_p, low α_g — is precisely designed to accommodate these cases as 'different scientific facts,' not to exclude them."**

*Why this does not suffice.* §3.F is a direct response to this reply. The exclusion argument depends on reading $\varphi$ narrowly as "theorem statement." Under the paper's own §5 (φ ranges over "claim, construction, algorithm, or empirical regularity") and §8.3 (Definition 2's machine-generation contribution is essentiality to "the first generation of the novel claim, construction, or method"), AlphaGeometry's auxiliary constructions, AlphaTensor's algorithms, and FunSearch's constructions are novel φ that machines generate. The refinement, correctly stated in the paper's own vocabulary — machine-essential contribution to the first generation of the novel claim, construction, or method $\varphi$, or to identifying the novel structural step in $\pi$ — does not exclude these cases. It excludes the case the framework should exclude: a human-authored Mathlib theorem where machine contributions are limited to kernel verification and automated instantiation of human-designed strategies.

The reply therefore rests on a mismatch between the refinement the attack proposes and the refinement the defense refutes. Once the refinement is stated using the paper's own broader φ, the paradigm-case-exclusion argument does not go through. The reply's substantive content — that the multidimensional autonomy vector is designed to handle mixed generation/proof contributions — is correct and is one of the paper's genuine contributions. It is not, however, an argument against the refined Definition 1. It is an argument for a graded Definition 1 in which the threshold is stated over the autonomy vector rather than as a binary generator-identity gate — which is precisely surrender condition (e).

The α_p / α_g decomposition the reply invokes strengthens the attack rather than weakening it. If AlphaGeometry has "low α_g and high α_p" and is still to be counted as machine discovery, then the framework's operational definition of "machine discovery event" must reach cases where at least one autonomy-vector component is high — which is exactly what surrender condition (e) requests. The reply agrees with the attack's structural demand; it disagrees only about whether the requirement should be located in Definition 1 (the attack's option (a)) or in a specified Definition 2 threshold (the attack's option (e), which the reply implicitly endorses without saying so).

**Reply to 3.E: "The paper notes that recursive productivity is not required for discovery (§9). The LeanDojo evidence supports the infrastructure's recursive character, which is relevant to Definition 3's motivating context."**

*Why this does not suffice.* The paper's §19 formulation — "the discovery becomes recursive when that enlargement measurably changes what later learners can infer, solve, or discover" — specifically attributes the recursive consequence to a machine discovery event, not to certified epistemic expansion generally. If certified human-authored expansion (without machine generation of the expanding artifact) also measurably changes what later learners can do, the recursive productivity measure cannot attribute the effect specifically to machine discovery. The LeanDojo evidence establishes that the infrastructure for recursive epistemic expansion is in place and operational. It does not establish that machine-generated certified results specifically contribute to the recursive productivity that Definition 3 quantifies. The paper should distinguish: (a) the certified epistemic expansion infrastructure is recursively productive (the LeanDojo evidence establishes this), and (b) machine-originated certified expansions specifically improve recursive productivity (not yet established by the LeanDojo evidence).

**Reply to 3.F: "The paper's §3 cases are all genuinely φ-generation cases (in the broad §5/§8.3 sense), and this is the direct defense's actual argument (that machine contribution to construction/algorithm generation is genuine machine discovery). There is therefore no gap between what the paper covers and what the refinement would require."**

*Why this does not suffice.* This reply is a partial concession that inverts the framework-preserving defense's own logic. If the §3 cases are all cases where machines make essential contribution to generating φ in the broad sense (claim, construction, or method), then the refinement does not narrow the framework's coverage. If the refinement does not narrow the framework's coverage, then the framework-preserving defense's exclusion-of-paradigm-cases argument (its principal move) collapses. If that argument collapses, the only remaining reason to prefer the current Definition 1 over the refined Definition 1 is that the current formulation is easier to state (five conditions, no essentiality distinction). The attack's claim that Definition 1 fails to define "machine discovery event" — because it is satisfied by human-authored kernel-verified theorems, which are not §3 cases — remains untouched. The reply amounts to: "the framework covers the cases it covers correctly; it also covers cases it should not." This is not a defense; it is a restatement of the gap.

## 5. Scope of the Attack

This attack shows:

- Definition 1 captures **certified epistemic expansion events** — auditable transitions in a public epistemic state — and is satisfied by any Lean proof with adequate provenance, regardless of machine contribution to claim generation or to novel structural steps in the certificate.
- Condition 4's provenance modularity creates no minimum machine-contribution threshold distinguishing "machine discovery events" from "certified epistemic expansion events."
- The paper's §19 central-claim formulation is false as stated: it is satisfied by pipelines where the essential machine contribution is verification, with no machine contribution to the artifact's generation.
- The experimental program (§15.1) requires machine claim-generation as the starting point, revealing the scope that Definition 1 does not enforce.
- The automated components in Lean Mathlib (automated tactics, kernel verification) operate within human-specified strategy frameworks and do not generate the claim $\varphi$ or identify novel structural steps in the certificate — they instantiate human-designed strategies within bounded automated search.
- The LeanDojo recursive productivity evidence supports certified epistemic expansion's recursive benefit regardless of generator identity, not machine-discovery-specific recursive benefit.
- The framework-preserving defense of Definition 1's silence — that refinement would exclude the paradigm cases — depends on reading $\varphi$ narrowly as "theorem statement," when the paper's own §5 and §8.3 authorize the broader reading (claim, construction, or method) under which the refinement does not exclude the paradigm cases.
- The auditing/classification separation architectural defense concedes rather than defeats the attack: it splits the paper's central claim from the architecture's actual reach and requires either §19 to be rewritten or a Definition 2 threshold to be specified.

This attack does NOT show:

- That the five-condition framework is incorrect or without value. It correctly specifies the auditing requirements for certified epistemic expansion and is genuinely more rigorous than informal discovery definitions.
- That the auditing/classification separation is an architecturally bad design. It is an architecturally good design that is not underwritten by the framework's central-claim formulation as stated.
- That the LeanDojo evidence is methodologically flawed or non-probative. It correctly supports certified library growth's recursive benefit for later theorem-proving systems.
- That machine discovery events cannot occur in Lean Mathlib. Machine-generated claims (neural prover suggestions for unspecified goals, genuinely autonomous search outputs) do enter Mathlib, and if adequately documented, would satisfy a refined definition. The attack concerns the current rate and the definitional gap, not the impossibility.
- That Definitions 2 and 3, the experimental program (§15), and the falsifiable hypotheses (§16) are misguided. These are valuable and appropriately scoped research directions. The attack is confined to Definition 1's scope relative to the paper's central claim and to the absence of a Definition 2 threshold.
- That the paper's distinction between circular self-training and recursive discovery (§12.3) is incorrect. This distinction is correct and important, and the audit boundary in Mathlib implements it.

## 6. Surrender Conditions

**(a) Definitional adequacy.** The central attack is defeated if Definition 1 is revised to include a condition requiring machine-essential contribution to the first generation of the novel claim, construction, or method $\varphi$, or to identifying the novel structural step in the certificate $\pi$, rather than only to verifying $\pi$ for a human-specified $\varphi$ or to automated instantiation of a human-specified strategy. The refinement uses the paper's own §5 and §8.3 language for what $\varphi$ ranges over and preserves coverage of the paradigm cases (AlphaGeometry, AlphaTensor, FunSearch, Minimo — all involve machine-essential contribution to generating novel constructions, algorithms, or claims in the broad sense). It excludes the case the framework should exclude: human-authored Mathlib entries where machine contributions are limited to kernel verification and automated instantiation of human-designed tactic strategies.

**(b) Scope repositioning.** The attack is substantively defeated if the paper explicitly repositions Definition 1 as a framework for certified epistemic expansion (applicable uniformly to human and machine pipelines), within which machine discovery is classified by Definition 2's autonomy vector at a specified minimum threshold, and §19's central-claim formulation is revised to reflect this repositioning. The direct Definition 1 defense's architectural separation argument is best read as an argument *for* this repositioning: it establishes that Definition 1 is deliberately generator-agnostic, which is incompatible with §19 as currently formulated. The paper would then claim: a unified framework for tracking certified epistemic transitions, enabling both human and machine discovery claims to be evaluated against common standards and scored on a comparable autonomy vector. This is a genuine contribution. It is less than "machine discovery is $X$" (where $X$ is Definition 1) but more precisely specified and still advances over informal discovery discussions.

**(c) LeanDojo evidence specificity.** The recursive productivity attack (§3.E) is defeated if evidence is produced distinguishing the recursive productivity contribution of machine-generated Mathlib results from human-generated results of equivalent size. If machine-generated Mathlib entries (neural prover suggestions, automated conjecture outputs) produce disproportionate improvement in subsequent prover performance relative to their count, the recursive productivity evidence supports the machine discovery claim specifically, not only certified epistemic expansion. This requires the §18.3 provenance metadata improvements the paper recommends: without per-result tracking of machine vs. human generation, the LeanDojo performance differential cannot be attributed to machine-generated results specifically.

**(d) Autonomous claim-generation evidence in Mathlib.** The empirical component of §§3.C–3.D is defeated if evidence shows that machine components in current Mathlib contributions extend to claim-generation or to identifying novel structural steps beyond automated instantiation of human-designed strategies — systems generating novel $\varphi$ (in the broad sense) or novel structural steps in $\pi$ without human specification of the goal or the strategy — at non-trivial scale, with adequate provenance documentation. If neural provers are generating novel theorem statements or novel structural steps (not only proving human-specified statements within human-specified strategy spaces), these cases approach the §15.1 experimental paradigm and would satisfy a refined definition. The provenance gap the supportive acknowledges (§3.5 of `yesindeed/machine-discovery-mathlib-case.md`) is the primary obstacle: without per-theorem tracking of generation autonomy, the autonomous claim-generation cases cannot be isolated in the current Mathlib record.

**(e) Machine-origination threshold at Definition 2.** A structural defeat of the definitional attack (§§3.A–3.B) is available if the paper specifies a minimum Definition 2 autonomy level that is required for Definition 1 events to count as *machine discovery events* rather than *certified epistemic expansion events*. Without specifying this threshold, the category "machine discovery event" is coextensive with "certified epistemic expansion event with any essential machine contribution anywhere in the pipeline," which includes the human-authored kernel-verified Mathlib case. With the threshold specified — for example, minimum α_g above a stated floor, or minimum α_p above a stated floor when combined with novelty at the construction level — the machine discovery event category is non-trivially distinguished, and Condition 4 becomes a meaningful filter when paired with the threshold. This does not require revising Definition 1 itself but requires specifying what Definition 2 level is required for Definition 1 events to be machine discovery events rather than merely certified epistemic expansion events. The direct Definition 1 defense's architectural separation argument establishes that Definition 2 is the intended location for this threshold; the paper does not currently supply the threshold's value.

**(f) Field-standard convergence.** A conditional defeat is available if the field's operational usage of "machine discovery" converges on the current Definition 1's broad reading — such that kernel-verified human-authored theorems are routinely described in the literature as "machine discovery events" without conceptual objection. Under this convergence, the framework's broader Definition 1 scope would match field usage rather than depart from it, and the attack's definitional-precision claim would apply to the field's terminology rather than to the paper's framework specifically. Present usage does not support this convergence: AlphaGeometry, AlphaTensor, and FunSearch are consistently described as machine discovery cases specifically because the machine's contribution to generating the novel object (construction, algorithm) is essential, not because they pass a certification pipeline.
