## What I decided to argue

**Improvement to existing paper — new §3.8, additions to §4, §5, §6.**

The adversarial r7's attack structure turns on an inference that needs contesting at a precise level: the inference from "the Italian CPC 1940 separately codified both obligations" to "accountability was part of the transmitted *libero convincimento* vocabulary" requires two steps, and the adversarial's structural probability argument establishes step (a) (Liebman transmitted both obligations) but not step (b) (the accountability dimension was constituted as internal to *libero convincimento*'s positive designatum specifically).

**The core response: version (i*).**

The adversarial's version (i)/(ii) binary does not track the vocabulary absence claim's operative level:

- Version (i) [adversarial]: the Italian tradition lacked the accountability concept entirely — improbable given Art. 132.
- Version (ii) [adversarial]: the concept was present in the unified transmitted vocabulary but selectively not adopted — equals explained by legitimation incentives.

The operative claim is version (i*): *motivazione*'s accountability dimension was available in the Italian proceduralist vocabulary as a *separate procedural institution* (Art. 132's domain, its own scholarly debates on appellate reviewability and judgment-form adequacy), but was not constituted as an *internal positive component of the *libero convincimento* concept's positive designatum* in the scholarship organized around Art. 116 (evidentiary evaluation, judicial discretion over evidence weight, freedom from *prova legale* hierarchies).

**Why version (i*) is not version (ii):** Version (ii) requires that the accountability dimension was part of *libero convincimento* as a unified transmitted concept — available for selective exclusion. Under version (i*), the separation is located within the Italian tradition's own two-institution structure: the tradition transmitted *libero convincimento* (Art. 116) and *motivazione* (Art. 132) as analytically distinct institutions, and the C3 conceptual vocabulary-formation around *livre convencimento* drew on the former without necessarily integrating the latter as an internal conceptual component of the evidentiary evaluation principle specifically.

**Key structural argument:** The Italian CPC 1940's separate codification is itself evidence that the Italian proceduralist tradition treated the two obligations as analytically distinct. If accountability were so internally part of *libero convincimento*'s positive designatum that transmitting the principle necessarily transmitted the accountability dimension, the Italian codifiers would have had structural reason to express this unity through a single provision. They did not. The *libero convincimento* debates in Italian doctrine concern evidentiary discretion; the *motivazione* debates concern appellate reviewability. These are distinct scholarly areas.

**Against the adversarial's anticipated reply** (§4 of `otherwise/paper1g-transplant-specification.md`): The adversarial anticipates a response along these lines and argues that the reform tradition's *internal logic* requires unity — *motivazione* is "the structural counterpart that gives the freedom principle its rational character." This concerns the tradition's theoretical ideal. A theoretical ideal can require unity while the scholarly deployment of *libero convincimento* as a positive procedural principle remains organized around the Art. 116 evidentiary evaluation domain specifically. The code's two-institution structure is more direct evidence about how the tradition itself treated the relationship than the theoretical ideal is.

**Against §3.8.C (art. 93, IX CF evidence):** Under version (i*), *motivação* was available in the Brazilian proceduralist vocabulary as a separate judgment-form obligation — not internally part of *livre convencimento*'s positive designatum. The 1988 constitutionalization elevated that separate institution to constitutional status. The adversarial's reception-selectivity account requires selective reconstitution from within the concept; version (i*) requires no such reconstitution — the two institutions were always separate. The ongoing scope debates around art. 93, IX CF (including the ESHTR C2 debate) are more consistent with version (i*): if accountability had been fully internal to *livre convencimento*'s positive designatum, the constitutional provision's scope would be substantially resolved by the concept's content, not contested independently at the constitutional text level.

## What I considered and discarded

**Conceding §3.8.A and shifting entirely to the version (ii) terrain.** The adversarial's §4 anticipates this and argues the structural account explains selective non-adoption equally. Conceding would leave the supportive with the patrimonialism contribution compressed to exactly the terrain where the structural account is already well-positioned (legitimation incentives for selective non-adoption within a unified concept). Version (i*) is the more precise claim and it is genuinely supported by the two-institution structure of the Italian code. Conceding it without engaging version (i*) would be premature.

**Demanding primary-source evidence without providing a structural argument.** The supportive could simply say "the adversarial has not provided Liebman's USP course notes." This is true but not an argument — it would be correct procedure to note the burden but weak analysis that doesn't engage the structural probability case the adversarial has built from the code's structure. Version (i*) is the argument; the burden note is secondary.

**Arguing that Art. 132 was entirely unknown to Liebman's Brazilian audience.** This would be overreaching. The claim is not that *motivação* as a judgment-form obligation was absent from the Brazilian vocabulary — it clearly was not, since Brazilian courts were subject to judgment-form requirements. The claim is about the *libero convincimento* concept's positive designatum specifically, which is the more precise and defensible level.

## Assessment of the exchange after r7

The adversarial r7 has made a genuinely strong structural argument. The Italian CPC 1940's separate codification record is a verifiable historical fact, and the structural probability inference from it is sound. The adversarial's anticipated reply (internal logic of the reform tradition requiring unity) is also substantive.

The version (i*) response is the best available move because:
1. It accepts what the adversarial establishes (separate codification, institutional availability of accountability)
2. It contests the inference at the right level (internal integration into the *libero convincimento* concept's positive designatum specifically)
3. It finds the adversarial's two-institution structure argument as actually working for the supportive: if the Italian codifiers separated the two obligations into distinct Articles, this is evidence that the Italian proceduralist tradition itself treated them as analytically distinct.

What the adversarial's anticipated reply misses: the reply argues from the "internal logic of the reform tradition" at the level of the tradition's theoretical ideal, not from the specific *libero convincimento* scholarship's analytical treatment of the relationship between Art. 116 and Art. 132. These are different levels. The theoretical ideal may require unity; the scholarly deployment of the concept may remain organized around the Art. 116 domain specifically, with Art. 132 in a separate scholarly domain.

**Open after r7:** The question is now whether primary-source evidence from Italian *libero convincimento* scholarship specifically (not from the general reform tradition's theoretical framework) shows the two dimensions were treated as internally unified within the evidentiary evaluation concept's positive content. This is the falsification condition I've specified, and it gives the adversarial r8 a concrete path.
