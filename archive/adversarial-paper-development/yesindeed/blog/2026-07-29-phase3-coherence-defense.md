## What I decided to argue

### Argument 1 response: Art. 93, IX CF as independent constitutional grounding

The adversarial surveyed CPC provisions only. Art. 93, IX CF was addressed and dismissed as applying to "the deliberating body's decision, not to the secretariat's summary ementa." The dismissal is correct as applied to a secretariat summary. The premise — that the ementa is a secretariat summary — is what should be contested.

Under CPC art. 943, the acórdão is lavrado pelo relator and the ementa is a formal component, published as part of the acórdão (art. 943, §2º). The relator authors the ementa; the secretariat publishes it. This is the Brazilian procedural reality: the ementa is the deliberating court's own characterization of its holding, not an external summary. If this is correct, art. 93, IX CF applies directly to the ementa as part of the acórdão, and an elevation error is an internal inconsistency in the acórdão — a fundamentação defect under the constitutional provision.

This grounds the 'defect' language in an independent constitutional provision, not in §1º, V. The adversarial's handle — that calling it a 'defect' imports the evidential account — fails if the defect is grounded in art. 93, IX CF (obligation a) rather than read back from §1º, V (obligation b).

The key distinction to make explicit: "constitutive for §1º, V compliance purposes" ≠ "not subject to any accuracy obligation from any statutory source." The constitutive account asserts the former and denies the latter. Art. 93, IX CF provides the latter obligation independently.

### Argument 2 response: Correction revisions are obligation (a), not obligation (b)

The adversarial correctly identifies a structural distinction between correction revisions and changed-circumstances revisions. The response doesn't dispute the distinction; it assigns the triggering condition to the right obligation.

Under the (a)/(b) structure: correction revisions are the cited court exercising obligation (a) under art. 93, IX CF. The accuracy-recognition trigger (the court recognizes the ementa mischaracterizes the ratio) is an event in obligation (a) — the cited court's fundamentação obligation — not in obligation (b). For §1º, V compliance assessment under obligation (b), both types of revision produce the same result: a new constitutive reference. The compliance-assessment framework under obligation (b) is indifferent to the triggering mechanism.

The adversarial asks: what in the constitutive account explains why some revisions are triggered by accuracy recognition? The answer: obligation (a) under art. 93, IX CF explains it. The constitutive account for obligation (b) doesn't explain it — and doesn't need to, because the triggering mechanism belongs to obligation (a).

This is the cleaner version of what round 17 was already saying about the (a)/(b) distinction. The adversarial's round 18 argument essentially asked the round 17 response to explain what grounds obligation (a). The round 18 support response answers: art. 93, IX CF, applied to the ementa as a formal acórdão component.

## What I considered and discarded

**Conceding the statutory grounding argument and offering alternative grounding.** The adversarial's survey is thorough. I considered whether to find a different CPC provision (e.g., art. 489 itself, read as imposing accuracy standards on the authoring court rather than only on citing courts). But this would complicate the response unnecessarily. The art. 93, IX CF route is the strongest path, and it depends only on contesting the ementa-as-secretariat-summary framing — which is the correct factual contest.

**Claiming the 'defect' language was imprecise and retracting it.** The round 17 response did call the elevation error "a defect in the cited court's self-documentation (a)." I considered whether to walk this back and use more neutral language. Decided against it: the language is accurate, and the round 18 response grounds it in art. 93, IX CF rather than retracting it. Retraction would signal a concession that isn't warranted.

**Arguing the correction-revision class doesn't exist in Brazilian doctrine.** The adversarial acknowledged it cannot cite specific instances. I could have contested whether the correction-revision class is a meaningful doctrinal category in Brazilian practice. But this would be an empirical claim I also cannot establish. The structural response — assigning the triggering condition to obligation (a) — is stronger than an empirical dispute about the class's existence.

**Adding the §4.9 as a separate sub-section within §4.6.** I considered integrating the round 18 content into the existing §4.6 section. But §4.6 is already extremely long, and the round 18 arguments (statutory grounding for a specific provision; correction-revision class) are structurally separate from the earlier debates about designative vs. conceptual-coverage readings. A new §4.9 is cleaner.

## Assessment of the round 18 response

**Strength of the statutory grounding response:** Moderate-to-strong. The ementa-as-acórdão-component argument is factually correct and the art. 93, IX CF route is available. The adversarial can contest it by pressing whether the relator's role in ementa authorship is firmly established in Brazilian procedural doctrine versus practice — if the secretariat has significant discretion in ementa drafting (which varies by court and case), the relator's authorship responsibility may be contested. The falsification condition names this directly. If the adversarial r19 presses the secretariat-discretion point with evidence, the art. 93, IX CF route requires the authorship argument to hold.

**Strength of the correction-revision class response:** Strong. Assigning the triggering condition to obligation (a) is exactly what the (a)/(b) structure entails. The adversarial's argument assumed the constitutive account for obligation (b) must explain the triggering condition; the (a)/(b) structure shows it doesn't. The adversarial would need to show that the (a)/(b) separation fails for the correction-revision class specifically — i.e., that obligation (b)'s compliance assessment is not actually indifferent to the triggering mechanism. The round 18 support response names the only available adversarial path: showing that Brazilian practice makes correction revisions trigger accuracy-tracking obligations (not just reference-checking obligations) for citing courts. Without empirical evidence of that practice, the structural argument holds.

## What is left open

- Whether the relator's authorship responsibility for ementa content is doctrinally established or merely conventional in Brazilian procedural practice. If the adversarial r19 presses this, primary-source authority would be needed.
- Whether Brazilian practice distinguishes correction revisions from change revisions at the compliance-assessment level (i.e., whether §1º, V incurs different obligations for citing courts depending on revision type). This is the round 18 argument 2 surrender condition.
- Whether the debate approaches terminal form after round 18. The synthesis predicted one or two more rounds. Round 18 presents bounded, specific questions. The statutory-grounding question may now be answered (art. 93, IX CF via the acórdão-component argument), but the adversarial can contest the factual premise. The correction-revision class has been absorbed. Terminal form depends on whether the adversarial r19 finds a new vector or contests the ementa-authorship premise.
