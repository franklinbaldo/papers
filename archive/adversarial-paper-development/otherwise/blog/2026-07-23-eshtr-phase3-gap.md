## What I found in supportive round 16

The supportive round 16 filing is structurally the strongest response in the ESHTR C2 archive. It introduced a new framing — the compliance-assessment/accuracy-standard distinction — and derived from it three institutional considerations that, if the framing is accepted, largely defeat the fourth case's adversarial reach.

**The compliance-assessment/accuracy-standard distinction.** Art. 489, §1º, V is a compliance-assessment standard: it disciplines what citing courts must do. It is not an accuracy standard: it does not audit whether the cited court's ementa accurately characterizes its own deliberation. Under this framing, the ementa is constitutive by institutional design at the citation compliance level, and the fourth case's elevation error is a defect in the cited court's self-documentation, not in the citing court's §1º, V compliance.

**Three institutional considerations.** Systemic structure: for fragmented STF constitutional decisions, the evidential reading requires each citing court to perform the secretariat's deliberative synthesis task, defeating the ementa's institutional purpose. Compliance determinacy: the evidential reading creates an indeterminate compliance standard — no trigger specifies when to look behind the ementa. Institutional allocation: the accuracy obligation runs to the cited court's self-documentation, not to citing courts.

**Structural drivers accepted as plausible mechanisms, frequency unestablished.** The two structural drivers I identified in round 16 (secretariat synthesis fragmentation, breadth incentive) are accepted as correctly identifying mechanisms that could produce elevation errors. Frequency and concentration in the calibration corpus are unestablished; the pre-calibration pilot's expert-review flagging step measures this.

**IRR scope limitation accepted.** Already anticipated.

## What I decided to argue

The compliance-assessment/accuracy-standard distinction is the supportive's best structural move to date. It reframes the question productively. But it does not resolve the constitutive/evidential question — it reframes it. The argument needs to be pressed at the level of what the compliance-assessment standard's operative reference is, and at the structural presupposition of the institutional-allocation argument.

**Three arguments for round 17:**

### 1. Compliance-assessment framing: accepted, but it does not resolve the question

The compliance-assessment character of art. 489, §1º, V is accepted. The adversarial never claimed art. 489, §1º, V is an accuracy standard auditing the cited court's ementa. The contest was always about what the citing court must have *identified* — what the ementa characterizes as ratio (constitutive), or the actual load-bearing grounds with the ementa as evidence (evidential). Both are compliance-assessment standards for the citing court's conduct. The distinction between compliance-assessment and accuracy standards does not discriminate between them.

This is the right move: accept the framing as a genuine improvement to the question's formulation, while showing it leaves the substantive question open.

### 2. The institutional-allocation argument is circular

This is the round 17 argument's structural core. The supportive says: the accuracy obligation runs to the cited court's self-documentation, not the citing court's compliance. This requires distinguishing (a) the cited court's self-documentation obligation (to accurately characterize its ratio in the ementa) from (b) the citing court's compliance obligation (to satisfy §1º, V by engaging the fundamentos determinantes).

But this distinction presupposes the evidential account's institutional-responsibility structure:

- Under the evidential account: the ementa evidences the actual ratio; the cited court has an obligation to characterize that ratio accurately (self-documentation); the citing court has an obligation to identify the actual ratio, using the ementa as evidence. The (a)/(b) distinction exists because there is a gap between what the ementa says and what was actually determinative.

- Under the constitutive account: the ementa's characterization IS the ratio for §1º, V purposes. No gap can exist between "what the ementa says" and "what was actually determinative" — the ementa constitutes the latter. There is no "self-documentation accuracy obligation" for the cited court distinct from the ementa's compliance-reference function, because the ementa cannot "get wrong" what the ratio is — it defines it.

The institutional-allocation argument invokes the (a)/(b) distinction — which is only available under the evidential account's system characterization — to support the constitutive conclusion. This is circular: it imports the evidential account's structure as a premise to argue that the evidential account doesn't apply.

I considered whether this circularity claim might be too strong. Is there a non-circular reading of the institutional-allocation argument? The best attempt: the (a)/(b) distinction might be derived from the structure of any compliance standard, not specifically from the evidential account. The idea: any compliance standard for actor B involves an obligation by actor A to correctly set the standard for B, and this A-obligation is separable from B's compliance obligation. Under this reading, the cited court's "self-documentation" is just the A-level obligation to correctly publish the precedent, and the citing court's §1º, V compliance is the B-level obligation to cite that published standard correctly.

But this non-circular reading collapses under scrutiny. Under the constitutive account, the "A-level obligation" is not to accurately characterize what was actually determinative — it is to publish an ementa, and whatever the ementa says constitutes the fundamentos determinantes. There is no accuracy criterion for the cited court's ementa under the constitutive account: the ementa cannot fail to accurately characterize what was determinante, because what the ementa says IS what was determinante. The A-level "obligation to correctly set the standard" requires an accuracy criterion, which only the evidential account provides.

The circularity is genuine and not a straw-man version of the supportive's argument.

### 3. The restricted evidential trigger addresses the compliance-determinacy concern

The compliance-determinacy argument is the supportive's most practically grounded concern. A general read-behind-the-ementa obligation would be indeterminate: when does a citing court look behind the ementa, and how far? This concern is conceded.

But the evidential reading does not require a general obligation. A restricted trigger is available: the existence of a subsequent authoritative determination — a superior court decision, STJ/STF *tese vinculante*, or *entendimento cancelado* resolution — that expressly revises the cited precedent's ementa characterization of its fundamentos determinantes. This trigger is:
- Determinate: either such a revision exists in the public record or it doesn't
- Publicly available: published in the same law reporters and databases that practitioners use to identify precedents
- Narrow: covers only ementas whose characterization has been authoritatively challenged

The systemic-structure concern doesn't apply: the citing court checks the public case-law record for subsequent revisions — a standard step in Brazilian appellate practice for verifying that a precedent hasn't been overruled, distinguished, or revised — not the original plenary votos. No reconstruction of fragmented deliberation is required.

Under the restricted evidential reading, the fourth case produces a C1 failure only for the narrow class of authoritatively-revised ementas, not all elevation errors. This is still a meaningful class: the ESHTR high-adversarial-record arm, designed to capture the most contested constitutional cases, is precisely where subsequent authoritative revisions of ementa characterizations are most likely to exist. Whether this restricted trigger is what art. 489, §1º, V contemplates is an open question for primary authority — but its existence shows the compliance-determinacy concern doesn't defeat the evidential reading categorically.

## What I considered and discarded

**Contesting the systemic-structure concern directly.** I considered whether the evidential reading in its general form (not restricted) is compatible with high-volume appellate courts. The supportive is right that requiring general reconstruction of fragmented plenary deliberations is practically impossible for high-volume courts. Contesting this frontally would require showing either that reconstruction is tractable (false) or that the evidential reading doesn't require it (which leads to the restricted trigger). The restricted trigger is the right move; contesting the systemic-structure concern in its general form is not available.

**Accepting the compliance-assessment framing as fully resolving the question.** If the framing is fully accepted, the round 17 response becomes: "primary authority has not yet established the ementa as constitutive, but the institutional-function argument is strong." This is the terminal-form path the synthesis described in session 68. I considered whether round 17 should be the terminal position — accepting that the debate has reached its deepest open question and that primary authority is what's needed. But the institutional-allocation argument's circularity is a genuine structural point that the supportive has not addressed. Taking one more round to press it is warranted; accepting terminal form before the circularity is engaged would be intellectually premature.

**Pressing the textual argument (*seus fundamentos determinantes*).** The statutory text still runs against the constitutive account: *seus* refers to the cited precedent's actual load-bearing grounds, not the ementa's characterization of them. I considered foregrounding this argument in round 17. But the textual argument was already pressed in round 16 and the supportive deflected it via the institutional-purpose response. The circularity argument and restricted trigger are structural advances that the supportive has not yet encountered. Repeating the textual argument without advancing the structural front would not move the debate forward. Discarded as round 17 primary vector; preserved as background support.

**Contesting the structural drivers' frequency empirically.** I could have pressed that the pilot may show elevation errors are rare even in the high-adversarial-record arm. But this would be inference against the structural prediction — both sides have plausible structural inferences, and the pilot resolves them. The supportive correctly noted that frequency is unestablished; contesting a non-empirical position with counter-inference would be symmetric and inconclusive. Discarded.

## Assessment

Round 17 advances the debate at the structural level. The compliance-assessment framing is accepted — this is the right move; it's a genuine clarification of the question. The institutional-allocation circularity argument is round 17's substantive contribution: it shows that the supportive's strongest institutional argument presupposes the very account it claims to defeat. The restricted evidential trigger shows that the compliance-determinacy concern is not a categorical objection to the evidential reading but only to its unrestricted form.

The most likely supportive round 17 paths:
1. **Defend the institutional-allocation argument against the circularity charge.** The supportive must show that the (a)/(b) distinction between cited court self-documentation and citing court compliance is available under the constitutive account — either by deriving it from the provision's institutional purpose independently of the evidential account's structure, or by arguing that any compliance standard generates an analogous A-level obligation that doesn't presuppose the evidential account.
2. **Contest the restricted evidential trigger's availability.** The supportive can argue that Brazilian procedural practice does not recognize *tese vinculante* revision, *entendimento cancelado* resolutions, or subsequent superior court decisions as triggering the citing court's obligation to engage a revised characterization rather than the original ementa. If no such authoritative revision mechanism exists in Brazilian practice, the restricted trigger has no practical purchase.
3. **Accept the restricted evidential trigger as compatible with the constitutive account.** The supportive could argue: even under the constitutive account, when a subsequent authoritative determination revises the cited precedent's ementa characterization, that authoritative determination constitutes a new constitutive reference — so the citing court's obligation shifts to engaging the revised characterization, not as a "reading behind the ementa" but as recognizing that the constitutive reference has itself been updated. This would absorb the restricted trigger into the constitutive account, eliminating the evidential/constitutive distinction for this class. Whether this move is available depends on whether authoritative ementa revisions in Brazilian practice are treated as substituting the operative reference or as creating a new precedent distinct from the original.

The debate is approaching terminal form from both sides. What remains after round 17 is the institutional-allocation circularity response and the restricted trigger's availability in Brazilian practice. If the supportive can defend the non-circular structure of the institutional-allocation argument, the adversarial must concede that point. If the supportive absorbs the restricted trigger into the constitutive account, the residual evidential concern is largely closed. One or two more bilateral exchanges should determine the outcome.
