"The adversarial's narrowed path: the C1/C4 statutory distinction does not distribute acknowledgment-checking to C1 and correctness-assessment to C4 cleanly if *identificar* is understood as requiring the annotator to determine whether the specific doctrinal construction is the kind of application the abstract principle covers — a conceptual coverage judgment. On this reading, *identificar* embeds a principle-to-application inference that crosses the specificity gap even if the annotator never asks 'is this construction correct.' The adversarial's round 15 should test whether this conceptual coverage reading of *identificar* is textually available or whether the word is purely designative (is the abstract principle named in the citing court's text?). If C1 required correctness-assessment, C4 would be redundant for binding precedent invocations. Two-case analysis: (a) no principle named → C1 fails; (b) principle named → C1 satisfied, application correctness handled by C4. Falsification condition: arm-specific C1 IRR lower for acknowledgment-present-but-application-contested cases would falsify the acknowledgment-checking account.

## What I found in supportive round 14

**C1/C4 structural distinction.** The support's core structural move is: Art. 489, §1º, V (*identificar*) and Art. 927, §1º (correct application, distinction, justified deviation) are structurally distinct functions. If identification (C1) required correctness-assessment, C4 would be redundant for binding precedents. Therefore C1 is designative, not correctness-assessing. The statute's two-provision structure is invoked as the ground for limiting C1 to acknowledgment-checking.

**Two-case analysis.** (a) Citing court names no principle from the ementa → C1 fails. Annotator checks: is abstract principle P present in the text? No → C1 failure. Text-level observation. (b) Citing court names principle P → C1 satisfied. Whether the specific construction implementing P is correct → C4.

**IRR falsification condition.** If annotators systematically disagree on C1 for cases where the abstract principle is acknowledged but the specific application is contested — diverging based on application scope rather than acknowledgment presence — the acknowledgment-checking account fails.

---

## What I decided to argue

**Accepted the C1/C4 structural distinction as formally valid.** The support is right that Art. 489, §1º, V (*identificar*) and Art. 927, §1º (correct application, distinction, justified deviation) are non-redundant functions. Accepting this was the honest move: the structural observation is accurate and I cannot contest it without contradicting the statute's two-provision structure. C1 and C4 remain non-redundant on both the designative and conceptual-coverage reading of *identificar* — the distinction is not the load-bearing target.

**Contested the purely designative reading of *identificar* on two statutory grounds.**

*First: possessive *seus* in Art. 489, §1º, V.* The provision's failure condition is "sem identificar *seus* fundamentos determinantes" — "without identifying ITS fundamentos determinantes." The possessive *seus* refers to the cited precedent's actual fundamentos determinantes. This formulation implies that the fundamentos determinantes are an objective feature of the cited precedent that the citing court must correctly recognize — not merely any named principle. A purely designative reading accepts any named principle as satisfying the identification requirement without testing whether what was named is in fact one of the cited precedent's fundamentos determinantes. The possessive resists this.

*Second: Art. 489, §1º, VI's parallel *que justifiquem* formulation.* Art. 489, §1º, VI's *identificar* appears with a *que justifiquem* clause: "sem identificar os fundamentos determinantes do precedente *que justifiquem a aplicação ao caso sob julgamento*." The *que justifiquem* specifies that identified grounds must actually justify the case-specific application — substantive content, not mere naming. V and VI are independent failure conditions within the same legal sentence; V's *identificar* must carry its own content independent of VI's *que justifiquem* clause. If V's *identificar* were purely designative, V's failure condition would be exhausted by the mere absence of any principle name — a thin requirement that would not distinguish a decision that nominates the wrong grounds from one that names no grounds at all. VI's non-designative *identificar* establishes by structural analogy that V's *identificar* also carries substantive identification content.

**Identified a third case the purely designative reading misses.** The supportive's two-case analysis covers: (a) no principle named → C1 fails; (b) principle named → C1 satisfied. A third case: the citing court names principle P from the ementa but identifies a specific proposition as the fundamento determinante that was in fact *obiter* in the cited precedent — P was genuinely present, but the specific construction the citing court nominates as the fundamento was not load-bearing for the holding. Under the purely designative reading: case (c) satisfies C1 (P was named). Under the statute's failure condition and on the conceptual-coverage reading: case (c) is precisely the paradigm C1 failure the provision targets — a court that has not identified the cited precedent's actual fundamentos determinantes but has nominated the wrong ground. The purely designative reading does not catch this.

**Confirmed C1/C4 non-redundancy on the conceptual coverage reading.** C1 (conceptual coverage) checks whether the identified ground IS a fundamento determinante of the cited precedent — a question about the cited precedent's internal structure. C4 checks whether the correctly-identified fundamento was correctly applied to the present case — a question about the citing court's analysis. A court can fail C1 (nominate obiter) while performing correct C4 analysis on the misidentified ground. A court can pass C1 (correctly identify ratio) while misapplying it (fail C4). Non-redundancy holds on both readings.

**Accepted the IRR falsification condition as the correct discriminating test.** The conceptual coverage reading predicts exactly the acknowledgment-present-but-application-contested IRR divergence the supportive named as its falsification condition: in cases where P was named but an obiter proposition was nominated as the fundamento, annotators applying the conceptual coverage task will disagree on C1 precisely in those cases — because the acknowledgment of P alone does not settle whether the specifically-nominated construction was determinative. The purely designative reading predicts agreement in those cases (P was named → C1 satisfied). The IRR pattern that falsifies the acknowledgment-checking account is structurally predicted by the conceptual coverage reading.

## What I considered and discarded

**Contesting the C1/C4 structural distinction.** The distinction is in the enacted text. I cannot contest it without contradicting the statute itself. The honest move was accepting the distinction and locating the contest at the level of what *identificar* in V requires — not at the level of whether V and C4 are distinct.

**Accepting the acknowledgment-checking account for now and limiting the challenge to the ementa-interpretation difficulty.** This was not available without abandoning the statutory arguments. The possessive *seus* and the *que justifiquem* parallel are textually available arguments for the conceptual-coverage reading. Not pressing them would have been an intellectual concession not justified by the supportive's round 14 arguments.

**Pressing the argument as terminal.** The synthesis said round 15 may be terminal-forming or may continue the bilateral exchange. The correct approach was stating the arguments and letting the synthesis characterize the exchange. The adversarial does not self-characterize outcomes.

## Assessment

The round 14 acknowledgment-checking account is the strongest structural argument the supportive has filed in the ESHTR C2 archive. The C1/C4 distinction is genuinely derived from the statute's two-provision structure, and the two-case analysis cleanly demonstrates tractability on the purely designative reading.

Round 15's response is focused: the C1/C4 distinction is accepted, the contest is at the designative vs. conceptual-coverage level, two statutory grounds support the conceptual-coverage reading, and the IRR falsification condition is accepted as the correct empirical arbiter.

The supportive's path for round 15 response is constrained: either (a) show that the possessive *seus* in Art. 489, §1º, V is not carrying objective-set-recognition content — that "seus fundamentos determinantes" means only "the fundamentos determinantes it has been said to have" rather than "the fundamentos determinantes it actually has"; or (b) show that VI's *que justifiquem* clause does not establish that *identificar* in the preceding V carries non-designative content — that V's independent failure condition operates on the purely designative threshold despite VI's adjacent formulation; or (c) show that the third case (obiter nominated as fundamento determinante) is correctly handled by the purely designative reading because VI rather than V catches that failure.

If none of these paths is available, the conceptual-coverage reading is textually supported, the annotation-task challenge survives at step 2, and the pre-calibration pilot's arm-specific C1 IRR data is the empirical resolution mechanism.
